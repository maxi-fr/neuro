# Detecting Distribution Shift for a Learned Dynamics Model Used in MPC

## 1. Objective

The goal of this experiment is to determine whether state-action pairs encountered during MPC inference remain within the distribution supported by the data used to train a learned dynamics model.

The learned dynamics model is assumed to have the form

$$
x_{t+1} = f_\theta(x_t, u_t),
$$

where $x_t$ is the system state and $u_t$ is the control input.

The training dataset consists of trajectories generated using random-amplitude excitation signals $u(t)$. During deployment, however, the dynamics model is queried inside MPC, where the controller searches over candidate action sequences and recursively rolls the learned model forward.

The central question is therefore:

> Are the state-action pairs $(x,u)$ queried by MPC similar to the state-action pairs observed in the training data?

The first stage of the experiment deliberately avoids learned feature mappings, latent spaces, auxiliary uncertainty models, or additional neural networks. The analysis is performed directly in the original state-action space using distance-based statistical methods.

---

### 2. Why State-Action Coverage Matters

Random-amplitude excitation may provide broad marginal coverage of the control input $u$, but that does not guarantee uniform coverage of the joint state-action distribution.

The training data are generated by a behavior process that induces a joint distribution

$$
p_{\mathrm{train}}(x,u)
=
p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\,p_{\mathrm{train}}(u\mid x).
$$

Even if the control signal spans its admissible amplitude range, some combinations of state and control may occur rarely or not at all.

This matters for MPC because the optimizer is free to propose controls that differ systematically from the random excitation policy used during training. It may therefore produce combinations $(x,u)$ that are valid numerically but poorly supported by the training dataset.

The relevant object for distribution-shift detection is therefore the joint vector

$$
z_t =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_t \\
u_t
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The proposed experiment measures how far an MPC query point $z$ lies from the training data in this joint space.

---

### 3. Important Complication: Recursive MPC Rollouts

At time $t$, MPC evaluates candidate control sequences

$$
U = (u_t, u_{t+1}, \ldots, u_{t+H-1}).
$$

The learned dynamics model is recursively rolled forward:

$$
\hat{x}_{t+k+1}
=
f_\theta(\hat{x}_{t+k},u_{t+k}).
$$

Even if the measured state $x_t$ is well represented in the training data, later predicted states may drift away from the training distribution because model error compounds over the prediction horizon.

For this reason, distribution support should be evaluated at every point along the predicted MPC trajectory:

$$
(\hat{x}_{t},u_t),
(\hat{x}_{t+1},u_{t+1}),
\ldots,
(\hat{x}_{t+H-1},u_{t+H-1}).
$$

The experiment should therefore produce both pointwise and trajectory-level out-of-distribution diagnostics.

---

### 4. Preprocessing

#### 4.1 Construct state-action vectors

For every training transition, construct

$$
z_i =
\begin{bmatrix}
x_i \\
u_i
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The same construction is used for validation data and MPC rollout points.

##### 4.2 Normalize every dimension

Distance calculations should not be performed directly on the raw variables unless all state and action dimensions already have comparable scales.

For each dimension $j$, estimate the mean and standard deviation from the training reference set:

$$
\mu_j = \mathbb{E}[z_j],
\qquad
\sigma_j = \operatorname{std}(z_j).
$$

Then standardize all points using

$$
\tilde z_j
=
\frac{z_j-\mu_j}{\sigma_j}.
$$

The same $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ must be used for training, validation, and MPC data.

This step is essential because otherwise dimensions with large numerical ranges dominate the Euclidean distance.

---

### 5. Dataset Split

The available training trajectories should be divided at the trajectory level rather than by randomly splitting individual time steps.

A recommended split is:

- **Reference set:** used to define the support of the training distribution and build the nearest-neighbor index.
- **ID calibration set:** held-out trajectories generated using the same random-amplitude procedure as the training data.
- **Optional test trajectories:** held out for later analysis of model prediction error versus distribution-shift score.

Splitting whole trajectories avoids excessive temporal leakage between the reference and calibration sets.

---

### 6. Primary Metric: k-Nearest-Neighbor Distance

For a query point $z$, define its $K$-nearest-neighbor distance with respect to the reference dataset as

$$
d_K(z)
=
\frac{1}{K}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal N_K(z)}
\left\|
\tilde z-\tilde z_i
\right\|_2.
$$

Here, $\mathcal N_K(z)$ denotes the set of the $K$ nearest reference points.

Using several neighbors is preferred over only the single nearest neighbor because it reduces sensitivity to isolated or accidental close points in the training set.

A practical initial choice is to evaluate several values such as

$$
K \in \{5,10,20,50\}.
$$

The exact value does not need to be chosen theoretically. It can be selected based on stability of the resulting score and its relationship to prediction error.

---

### 7. Calibration of the k-NN Score

The absolute magnitude of $d_K$ is difficult to interpret in isolation, especially in high dimensions.

Instead, use the held-out ID calibration set to estimate the empirical distribution of k-NN distances:

$$
\{d_K(z_i^{\mathrm{cal}})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cal}}}.
$$

Define the empirical cumulative distribution function

$$
\hat F_{\mathrm{ID}}(d).
$$

For a new query point $z$, convert its raw distance to an ID percentile:

$$
q(z)
=
\hat F_{\mathrm{ID}}(d_K(z)).
$$

Interpretation:

- $q \approx 0.5$: typical of the held-out training distribution.
- $q \approx 0.95$: farther from the training reference set than approximately 95% of normal ID examples.
- $q \approx 0.99$: unusually far from the reference set relative to the calibration distribution.

This percentile representation is more interpretable than the raw k-NN distance and automatically adapts to the dimensionality and scale of the problem.

---

### 8. Applying the Metric Inside MPC

For each candidate MPC rollout, evaluate the k-NN score at every predicted state-action pair:

$$
q_k
=
q(\hat{x}_{t+k},u_{t+k}),
\qquad
k=0,\ldots,H-1.
$$

This produces a sequence

$$
q_0,q_1,\ldots,q_{H-1}.
$$

Two useful trajectory-level summaries are:

#### Maximum support violation

$$
Q_{\max}
=
\max_k q_k.
$$

This answers:

> Does the candidate rollout contain at least one strongly unsupported state-action pair?

##### Mean distribution-shift score

$$
Q_{\mathrm{mean}}
=
\frac{1}{H}
\sum_{k=0}^{H-1} q_k.
$$

This answers:

> Is the candidate rollout systematically far from the training distribution?

The complete sequence $q_k$ should also be retained because the dependence on prediction horizon is important.

---

### 9. Main Experiment

#### Step 1: Build the reference database

Collect all normalized state-action vectors from the reference trajectories:

$$
\mathcal Z_{\mathrm{ref}}
=
\{\tilde z_i\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}.
$$

Build a nearest-neighbor search structure over this dataset.

Depending on dataset size, possible implementations include a brute-force search, KD-tree, ball tree, or an approximate-nearest-neighbor implementation.

##### Step 2: Establish the in-distribution baseline

For every point in the ID calibration trajectories:

1. Construct $z=[x,u]$.
2. Normalize it using the reference-set statistics.
3. Compute $d_K(z)$.
4. Store the resulting distance.

These distances define the empirical ID reference distribution.

##### Step 3: Evaluate MPC rollouts

For every state-action pair considered along MPC predicted trajectories:

1. Construct $z=[\hat x,u]$.
2. Normalize it with the same statistics.
3. Compute $d_K(z)$.
4. Convert it to its ID percentile $q(z)$.
5. Record its position in the MPC horizon.

##### Step 4: Compare the distributions

Compare

$$
d_K(\text{MPC})
$$

against

$$
d_K(\text{ID calibration}).
$$

The central diagnostic is whether MPC produces systematically larger distances than held-out trajectories generated by the original random-excitation procedure.

In percentile form, inspect how frequently MPC rollout points exceed thresholds such as

$$
q > 0.95
$$

or

$$
q > 0.99.
$$

These are not automatically failure thresholds. They are statistical indicators that a query is unusual relative to the data-generation process used during training.

---

### 10. Horizon-Dependent Analysis

Because the dynamics model is recursively applied during MPC, the OOD score should be analyzed as a function of the prediction horizon.

For each horizon position $k$, calculate statistics such as

$$
\mathbb E[q_k],
\qquad
\operatorname{median}(q_k),
\qquad
P(q_k > 0.95),
\qquad
P(q_k > 0.99).
$$

This can reveal whether the model starts close to the training distribution but progressively leaves it as the rollout proceeds.

An important outcome of the experiment may therefore be an empirical relationship of the form

$$
k \uparrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
q_k \uparrow.
$$

If this occurs consistently, it indicates that long-horizon predictions are being evaluated in increasingly unsupported regions of state-action space.

---

### 11. Secondary Analytical Baseline: Standardized Global Distance

As an additional baseline, compute a diagonal Mahalanobis-like score:

$$
d_{\mathrm{diag}}(z)^2
=
\sum_j
\frac{(z_j-\mu_j)^2}{\sigma_j^2}.
$$

After standardization, this is equivalent to the squared Euclidean distance from the global training mean.

This metric ignores correlations between dimensions and does not capture local support as well as k-NN, but it is computationally cheap and provides a useful sanity check.

A full Mahalanobis distance could also be considered:

$$
d_M(z)^2
=
(z-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(z-\mu),
$$

but for very high-dimensional state-action spaces the covariance matrix may be poorly conditioned or expensive to estimate reliably. For this reason, k-NN and the diagonal standardized distance are preferable first baselines.

---

### 12. Check Whether High Dimensionality Is Actually a Problem

High dimensionality can cause pairwise distances to concentrate. The correct response is not necessarily to abandon raw-space k-NN, but to measure whether distance concentration is severe enough to make the score uninformative.

On the held-out ID calibration set, inspect the distribution of $d_K$.

A simple diagnostic is the coefficient of variation:

$$
CV
=
\frac{\operatorname{std}(d_K)}
{\operatorname{mean}(d_K)}.
$$

If the k-NN distances still exhibit meaningful spread across the calibration set, the metric remains capable of distinguishing relatively dense from relatively sparse regions.

If all distances become nearly identical, k-NN will provide little discrimination and a different metric may eventually be necessary.

This should be treated as an empirical question rather than assumed solely from the dimensionality of $x$ and $u$.

---

### 13. Validation Against Actual Dynamics-Model Error

Distribution shift is only useful operationally if it is related to model reliability.

On held-out real trajectories, compute the one-step model prediction error

$$
e_t
=
\left\|
f_\theta(x_t,u_t)-x_{t+1}
\right\|.
$$

For the same point, compute its k-NN score

$$
d_t=d_K(x_t,u_t)
$$

or calibrated percentile

$$
q_t=q(x_t,u_t).
$$

Then examine whether prediction error increases with OOD score:

$$
q_t \uparrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
e_t \uparrow.
$$

One simple evaluation is to divide the calibration/test samples into percentile bins and compute the prediction error in each bin.

For example:

| ID percentile range | Mean model error |
|---|---:|
| 0--50% | ... |
| 50--75% | ... |
| 75--90% | ... |
| 90--95% | ... |
| 95--99% | ... |
| 99--100% | ... |

A useful OOD metric should ideally show increasing model error as the support score becomes more extreme.

---

### 14. Multi-Step Validation

Because MPC uses recursive model predictions, one-step validation alone is not sufficient.

Take held-out real trajectories and initialize the learned model from a true state $x_t$. Then recursively roll it forward using the actual recorded control inputs:

$$
\hat{x}_{t+1}=f_\theta(x_t,u_t),
$$

$$
\hat{x}_{t+2}=f_\theta(\hat{x}_{t+1},u_{t+1}),
$$

and so on.

Compare the predicted state to the real state:

$$
e_{t,k}
=
\|\hat{x}_{t+k}-x_{t+k}\|.
$$

At the same time, compute the k-NN percentile along the model rollout:

$$
q_{t,k}
=
q(\hat{x}_{t+k},u_{t+k}).
$$

This allows direct investigation of whether increasing distance from the training support predicts increasing rollout error.

Useful rollout-level quantities include

$$
E_H
=
\max_{k\le H}e_{t,k},
$$

and

$$
Q_H
=
\max_{k\le H}q_{t,k}.
$$

The empirical relationship between $Q_H$ and $E_H$ is especially relevant for deciding whether the score should later be used inside the controller.

---

### 15. Initial Success Criteria

The first-stage experiment is successful if it can answer the following questions clearly:

1. Do MPC state-action queries have larger k-NN distances than held-out trajectories generated from the original random-excitation process?
2. Does the k-NN percentile increase as the MPC prediction horizon increases?
3. How frequently do MPC queries exceed the 95th or 99th percentile of the ID reference distribution?
4. Does a high k-NN/OOD score correlate with larger one-step model error?
5. Does a high score along a rollout correlate with larger multi-step prediction error?
6. Are the k-NN distances sufficiently dispersed to remain informative despite the dimensionality of $x$ and $u$?

These questions should be answered before introducing more complex OOD-detection methods.

---

## Follow-Up / Secondary Step: Closing the Data-Collection Loop

If the first-stage experiment shows that MPC frequently enters poorly supported regions and that the OOD score correlates with prediction error, the next step is to use this information to improve the training distribution.

The overall loop is:

$$
\boxed{\text{collect trajectories}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{train dynamics model}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{run MPC and generate candidate rollouts}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{evaluate state-action support with } d_K \text{ or } q}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{execute the selected first control action}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{collect the resulting real transition}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{add informative transitions to the dataset}}
$$

$$
\downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{retrain / update the dynamics model}}
$$

This is an iterative data-aggregation process: the controller exposes regions of state-action space that are relevant to the MPC policy, and new real transitions from those regions are incorporated into the dataset.

### 16.1 Data prioritization

Not every newly collected transition needs to be treated equally.

Transitions with unusually high k-NN percentile are especially informative because they expand coverage in regions that were previously sparse.

For example, a transition could be flagged for priority inclusion if

$$
q(x_t,u_t) > q_{\mathrm{threshold}}.
$$

The threshold can initially be chosen from the empirical ID distribution, for example 0.95 or 0.99, and later adjusted based on observed model error.

### 16.2 Retraining objective

After adding new transitions, recompute the normalization statistics and rebuild the k-NN reference database if the dataset changes materially.

Then retrain or update the dynamics model and repeat the same evaluation procedure.

A successful iteration should reduce the OOD score in regions that MPC repeatedly visits and improve prediction accuracy there.

### 16.3 Optional later use inside MPC

Only after validating that the OOD score reliably predicts model error should it be considered for direct use inside the MPC optimization.

One possibility is to augment the MPC objective:

$$
J(U)
=
\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}
\left[
\ell(\hat{x}_{t+k},u_{t+k})
+
\lambda\,s(\hat{x}_{t+k},u_{t+k})
\right],
$$

where $s$ may be the raw k-NN distance or a transformed/calibrated version of it.

Alternatively, one can impose a hard support constraint such as

$$
q(\hat{x}_{t+k},u_{t+k}) < q_{\max}
$$

for all rollout steps.

These controller modifications should be treated as a later stage. The first priority is to establish that the raw-space support metric is stable, interpretable, and correlated with dynamics-model error.

---

### 17. Recommended Order of Work

1. Split the random-excitation trajectories into reference and ID calibration sets.
2. Construct and standardize the joint state-action vectors $[x,u]$.
3. Build a k-nearest-neighbor index on the reference set.
4. Compute the empirical ID distribution of k-NN distances.
5. Convert distances to ID percentiles.
6. Evaluate these percentiles along MPC rollouts.
7. Analyze the score as a function of MPC horizon.
8. Check whether distance concentration makes the metric uninformative.
9. Validate the score against one-step model prediction error.
10. Validate it against multi-step rollout error.
11. If the score is meaningful, use MPC-generated real transitions to expand the dataset iteratively.
12. Only afterward consider incorporating the support score directly into the MPC objective or constraints.

---

### 18. Core Principle

The experiment should remain empirical and simple at the beginning.

The central comparison is

$$
\boxed{
\text{k-NN distance of MPC queries}
\quad\text{vs.}\quad
\text{k-NN distance of held-out training-like queries}
}
$$

and the central validation question is

$$
\boxed{
\text{Does larger distance from the training data predict larger dynamics-model error?}
}
$$

If the answer to both questions is yes, the resulting score provides a practical measure of whether the learned dynamics model is being queried within the region supported by its training data.
