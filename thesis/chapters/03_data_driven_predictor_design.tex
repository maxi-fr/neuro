
\chapter{Data-Driven Predictor Design}
\label{ch:data_driven_predictor_design}


\ac{mpc} requires a model capable of predicting how seizure-related neural activity evolves in response to stimulation currents.
Chapter~\ref{ch:biological_plant_modeling} introduced the biophysical plant used for simulation and evaluation in this thesis.
However, its governing equations, parameters, and internal neural states are assumed to be unavailable to the controller, which instead has access only to measured scalp \ac{eeg} signals and applied stimulation currents.
One possible approach is to identify the underlying mechanistic dynamics from these measurements.
However, parameter identification in brain network models is challenging near bifurcations, where the best agreement with empirical observations is often found.
As discussed in~\cite{pille2025tvboptim}, \ac{bptt} can produce exploding gradients in these regimes, making gradient-based identification unstable. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
This motivates the use of data-driven input-output models that learn the relationship between stimulation currents and \ac{eeg} observations without requiring explicit identification of the underlying biophysical dynamics.
This also avoids the need for state estimation.

This chapter investigates two prediction targets: multichannel \ac{eeg} waveforms and spectral power features derived from the \ac{stft}.
Waveform prediction retains the phase and temporal structure of the measured signals, whereas spectral power prediction describes their evolving frequency content without requiring the precise phase evolution of individual oscillations to be reproduced.
The latter is particularly relevant for representing seizure-related oscillatory activity and allows predictions to be evaluated directly using spectral control objectives.

Three families of data-driven models are investigated for these prediction tasks.
\Acp{mlp} provide a general-purpose nonlinear approach to learning input-output relationships from finite histories of observations and stimulation currents.
\Acp{cnn} exploit local structure through shared filters, allowing temporal patterns in \ac{eeg} waveforms or time-frequency patterns in spectral representations to be captured.
Finally, Hankel \ac{dmdc} provides a linear approach based on delay-embedded observations.
These architectures differ in their assumptions about the underlying input-output dynamics and provide alternative ways of representing the response to stimulation.

The chapter first formulates the input-output prediction problem and identifies the requirements relevant to seizure control.
It then introduces the waveform and spectral prediction targets, followed by the supervised learning framework, training data, training objectives, and predictor architectures.
The resulting prediction models are subsequently used in the \ac{mpc} framework developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:mpc_framework}.



\section{Prediction Problem and Targets}
\label{sec:prediction_problem}

This section establishes a common input-output prediction formulation for waveform and spectral observables.
It then introduces the construction of spectral prediction targets and discusses the trade-offs between the two representations.

\subsection{Input-Output Prediction Formulation}

Let $\mathbf{y}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$ denote the observable neural activity at discrete time step $k$, and let $\mathbf{u}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$ denote the vector of stimulation currents applied during the transition from $k$ to $k+1$, where $P$ is the number of stimulation electrodes.
Depending on the prediction target, $\mathbf{y}_k$ represents either multichannel \ac{eeg} samples or spectral power features derived from the \ac{eeg}.
Accordingly, one prediction step corresponds to either one sample of the downsampled waveform or one hop of the \ac{stft}.
In both cases, the stimulation current $\mathbf{u}_k$ is held constant over the corresponding interval.
Each spectral frame is timestamped by the latest \ac{eeg} sample needed to compute it and uses only measurements available at that time.

Since the internal neural states of the biophysical plant are not directly observable, predictions are conditioned on finite histories of measured outputs and previously applied stimulation currents, following the input-output identification approach described in~\cite[ch.~5]{Ljung1999}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
We define the history vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}_k =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf{y}_{k-d_\mathrm{y}+1}\\
        \vdots\\
        \mathbf{y}_k\\
        \mathbf{u}_{k-d_\mathrm{u}}\\
        \vdots\\
        \mathbf{u}_{k-1}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{d_\mathrm{y} n_y + d_\mathrm{u} P}\,,
    \label{eq:predictor_history}
\end{equation}
where $d_\mathrm{y}$ and $d_\mathrm{u}$ denote the observation and input history lengths, respectively.
The histories in~\eqref{eq:predictor_history} provide information about the influence of past neural activity and stimulation on the current dynamics, without requiring explicit estimation of the underlying physiological state.

The one-step input-output prediction problem is formulated as
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1}
    =
    \mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \left(\mathbf{h}_k,\mathbf{u}_k\right)\,,
    \label{eq:one_step_predictor}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ denotes a prediction model parameterized by $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1}$ is the predicted observable one step ahead of step $k$, given the information available at that step.
The hat distinguishes this prediction from the measured observable $\mathbf{y}_{k+1}$.
The current observation $\mathbf{y}_k$ and previously applied currents are known when the prediction is made, while $\mathbf{u}_k$ specifies the stimulation applied over the upcoming interval.

Two alternative parameterizations of~\eqref{eq:one_step_predictor} are considered.
In \emph{direct prediction}, the model estimates the next observable $\mathbf{y}_{k+1}$.
In \emph{residual prediction}, the model instead estimates the increment relative to the latest observation, such that
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \left(\mathbf{h}_k,\mathbf{u}_k\right)
    =
    \mathbf{y}_k
    +
    \mathbf{g}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \left(\mathbf{h}_k,\mathbf{u}_k\right)\,,
    \label{eq:residual_predictor}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{g}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ predicts the change $\mathbf{y}_{k+1}-\mathbf{y}_k$.
Thus, the direct formulation and the residual formulation in~\eqref{eq:residual_predictor} differ in their supervised learning targets, but both produce predictions in the original observable space.
Their relative performance is evaluated experimentally.

Although the models are formulated as one-step predictors, multi-step forecasts are obtained by recursively applying the learned mapping under a sequence of candidate stimulation currents.
Let $H$ denote the prediction horizon length and $j$ the step offset within the horizon.
For recursive prediction, $\mathbf{y}_{j|k}$ denotes the observable predicted $j$ steps ahead using the information available at step $k$.
Such that $\mathbf{y}_{1|k}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1}$.
Starting from the measured history $\mathbf{h}_{0|k}=\mathbf{h}_k$, the prediction $j+1$ steps ahead is given by
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}_{j+1|k}
    =
    \mathbf{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \left(
        \mathbf{h}_{j|k},
        \mathbf{u}_{j|k}
    \right),
    \qquad j=0,\ldots,H-1\,,
    \label{eq:recursive_predictor}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u}_{j|k}$ is the candidate stimulation current for the interval from $k+j$ to $k+j+1$, and $\mathbf{h}_{j|k}$ is the corresponding history vector.
Equation~\eqref{eq:recursive_predictor} applies the same learned one-step function at every step.
After each prediction, the history is updated by appending the newly predicted observable and the applied candidate current, while discarding the oldest entries.
In the residual formulation, each predicted increment is added to the latest predicted observable.

Since future measurements are unavailable during this recursive rollout, prediction errors can propagate and accumulate over the horizon.
Consequently, accurate one-step predictions do not necessarily guarantee accurate multi-step trajectories~\cite{Farina2011}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
The resulting forecasts provide the predicted system response to candidate stimulation sequences, which is subsequently used in the \ac{mpc} framework developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:mpc_framework}.


\subsection{Spectral Representation and \ac{stft}}
\label{sec:spectral_representation}

Seizure-related neural activity often manifests as changes in the amplitude and frequency content of ongoing oscillations.
Such changes can be characterized through spectral power without requiring the precise phase evolution of the underlying waveform to be reproduced.
Energy-based features have also shown discriminative value in \ac{eeg} classification, including epilepsy-related tasks, although their usefulness for prediction under stimulation remains to be established~\cite{Chourdaki2026}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
Spectral prediction therefore offers an alternative to modeling individual \ac{eeg} samples.
However, unlike waveform observations, spectral power must be estimated over finite measurement intervals.
The construction of these estimates determines which oscillatory features are retained, how variable they are, and how quickly changes in neural activity are reflected in the observable.

The \ac{stft} provides a time-frequency representation by applying the discrete Fourier transform to successive, potentially overlapping signal segments~\cite{Oppenheim2010}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
Let $x_{c,n}$ denote the preprocessed \ac{eeg} sample in channel $c$ at sample index $n$, acquired at frequency $f_\mathrm{s}$, with $M$ channels in total.
The sample index $n$ is distinct from the prediction-step index $k$ introduced in the preceding subsection.
For a segment length $L_\mathrm{s}$ and hop length $H_\mathrm{s}$, both expressed in samples, define the segment vector
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{q,c} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{c,qH_\mathrm{s}} &
        \cdots &
        x_{c,qH_\mathrm{s}+L_\mathrm{s}-1}
    \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\,,
\end{equation}
where $q$ indexes consecutive spectral frames.
Given a taper $\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_\mathrm{s}}$, the corresponding Fourier coefficients are
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}_{q,c}
    = \mathcal{F}_{+}
    \left(\mathbf{w}\odot\mathbf{x}_{q,c}\right)\,,
    \label{eq:causal_stft}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication and $\mathcal{F}_{+}$ retains the nonnegative-frequency coefficients of the $L_\mathrm{s}$-point discrete Fourier transform.
The coefficient at frequency-bin index $m$ is denoted by $X_{q,c,m}$.
For real-valued signals, these coefficients correspond to $m=0,\ldots,\lfloor L_\mathrm{s}/2\rfloor$, with frequencies
\begin{equation}
    f_m=\frac{mf_\mathrm{s}}{L_\mathrm{s}},
    \qquad
    \Delta f=\frac{f_\mathrm{s}}{L_\mathrm{s}}\,.
    \label{eq:stft_frequency_grid}
\end{equation}
Each complex coefficient contains a magnitude and phase; its squared magnitude describes the contribution of the corresponding frequency component to the segment's power.

The segment length, hop, and taper play different roles in the resulting representation.
Increasing $L_\mathrm{s}$ decreases the frequency-bin spacing $\Delta f$, allowing finer distinctions between neighboring oscillatory components, but also extends the interval over which their power is estimated.
Rapid changes can therefore be mixed with preceding activity within the same segment.
The hop $H_\mathrm{s}$ instead determines how frequently a new estimate becomes available, at intervals of $H_\mathrm{s}/f_\mathrm{s}$.
Overlapping segments, obtained when $H_\mathrm{s}<L_\mathrm{s}$, provide more frequent updates without changing the frequency grid.
However, consecutive estimates then share measurements and become more strongly correlated.
Thus, a smaller hop improves the temporal sampling of spectral activity without necessarily providing more independent information.

Tapering addresses a different limitation of finite segments: abrupt truncation introduces spectral leakage, spreading the contribution of an oscillation across neighboring frequencies.
The choice of taper determines both this spectral spreading and the relative importance assigned to measurements within the segment~\cite{Oppenheim2010}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
A symmetric Hann taper suppresses both segment boundaries and concentrates weight near the center, providing effective leakage reduction.
For a causally updated observable, however, this also means that the most recently acquired samples receive relatively little weight.
An alternative is a rising half-Hann taper, which gradually increases its weight from the oldest to the newest sample.
This makes the spectrum more sensitive to recent changes, potentially allowing emerging oscillatory activity to influence the observable sooner.
The trade-off is weaker suppression of the most recent segment boundary, which generally increases spectral leakage and sensitivity to short-lived fluctuations.
Neither choice is universally preferable: a symmetric taper favors spectral localization, whereas a recency-weighted taper favors responsiveness to evolving activity.
The relevant compromise depends on how quickly seizure-related changes must become observable and how reliably their frequency content can be distinguished from background fluctuations.

For causal processing, each spectral frame is associated with the latest sample required for its computation, defined by
\begin{equation}
    n_q=qH_\mathrm{s}+L_\mathrm{s}-1,
    \qquad
    t_q=\frac{n_q}{f_\mathrm{s}}\,.
    \label{eq:stft_frame_time}
\end{equation}
With this convention, frame $q$ contains no measurements acquired after $t_q$.
Its timestamp therefore reflects when the estimate can first be formed, rather than the center of the measurement interval.
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:stft_geometry}, successive frames represent overlapping portions of the waveform while remaining aligned with their causal availability.

For the prediction target, the Fourier coefficients are converted to one-sided, density-scaled power estimates given by
\begin{equation}
    S_{q,c,m}
    =
    \frac{a_m|X_{q,c,m}|^2}
    {f_\mathrm{s}\sum_{r=0}^{L_\mathrm{s}-1}w_r^2}\,,
    \label{eq:stft_power_density}
\end{equation}
where $w_r$ is the taper weight at sample offset $r$, $a_m=2$ for bins with a distinct negative-frequency counterpart, and $a_m=1$ at zero frequency and, for even $L_\mathrm{s}$, at the Nyquist frequency.
The denominator accounts for the sampling frequency and taper energy.
When the input is expressed in volts, the resulting density has units of volts squared per hertz.

The segment length determines the frequency resolution of the spectral estimate, with longer segments providing a finer frequency grid.
Nevertheless, increasing segment length does not, by itself, guarantee a lower-variance estimate at an individual frequency bin: periodograms remain inherently variable even for stationary signals~\cite{Astfalck2024}.
More importantly, longer segments combine activity over a longer interval.
In the present application, this creates a tension between obtaining reliable estimates of oscillatory power and retaining the temporal changes that may signal seizure onset or recruitment.

Additional variance reduction can be obtained by averaging power across consecutive spectral frames.
For a causal temporal kernel of length $L_\mathrm{k}$, with nonnegative weights $\alpha_\ell$ satisfying $\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_\mathrm{k}-1}\alpha_\ell=1$, define
\begin{equation}
    \overline{S}_{q,c,m}
    =
    \sum_{\ell=0}^{L_\mathrm{k}-1}
    \alpha_\ell S_{q-\ell,c,m}\,.
    \label{eq:stft_frame_smoothing}
\end{equation}
Here, $\ell=0$ refers to the latest frame, and $L_\mathrm{k}=1$ corresponds to no temporal smoothing.
For stationary activity, averaging several noisy power estimates generally reduces fluctuations.
If the estimates were independent and had equal variance, the variance of their weighted average would scale with $\sum_\ell\alpha_\ell^2$.
Overlapping \ac{stft} frames are correlated, however, so the reduction is generally smaller.

The temporal kernel also determines how strongly older activity influences the current observable.
Uniform weights provide stronger averaging for a given number of independent, equally variable estimates, but retain the contribution of older frames equally.
Recency-weighted kernels emphasize newer activity and can respond more quickly to changes, at the cost of reduced smoothing.
Increasing the kernel length further suppresses fluctuations, but may also dilute the early stages of a seizure-related change with preceding background activity.
Unlike increasing $L_\mathrm{s}$, temporal smoothing does not alter the frequency grid.

The spectral observable is finally expressed on a logarithmic scale.
This logarithmic representation is useful because changes in oscillatory power are often more naturally compared through relative rather than absolute differences.
The logarithm maps multiplicative changes in power to additive differences, while compressing the range of the resulting features.
To preserve the interpretation of $\overline{S}_{q,c,m}$ as power density, the transformation is applied after temporal smoothing, yielding
\begin{equation}
    z_{q,c,m}
    =
    \ln\left(
        \overline{S}_{q,c,m}+\varepsilon
    \right)\,,
    \label{eq:stft_log_power}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon>0$ is a small floor preventing undefined values at zero power.
Averaging precedes the logarithm because mean power and mean log-power are different quantities.
The decibel scale used in the figures is equivalent to a constant rescaling of the natural logarithm.

Stacking $z_{q,c,m}$ across the $M$ EEG channels and $N_\mathrm{b}$ retained frequency bins within the selected band defines the spectral observable $\mathbf{y}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{M N_\mathrm{b}}$, with prediction step $k$ corresponding to spectral frame $q$.
Each step therefore advances by $H_\mathrm{s}$ waveform samples.
Although the observable is updated at this rate, it summarizes a longer history of measurements.
A fully supported smoothed frame depends on
\begin{equation}
    L_\mathrm{support}
    =
    L_\mathrm{s}+(L_\mathrm{k}-1)H_\mathrm{s}\,,
    \label{eq:stft_sample_support}
\end{equation}
waveform samples, excluding any additional preprocessing history.
Constructing $d_\mathrm{y}$ consecutive spectral observations requires $L_\mathrm{support}+(d_\mathrm{y}-1)H_\mathrm{s}$ samples.
This history must be available to initialize the predictor, but thereafter each new frame requires only the next $H_\mathrm{s}$ samples.
Because the segment and smoothing kernel contain no future measurements, the resulting representation remains causal.

The length of this measurement history should not be interpreted as a fixed detection delay.
Once initialized, the next spectral frame can already reflect newly acquired samples, with their contribution determined by the taper and temporal kernel.
The time required for a seizure-related change to become distinguishable from background fluctuations consequently depends on its frequency content, magnitude, and the chosen processing parameters.

The resulting representation retains the evolution of frequency-specific power while discarding the Fourier phases.
It is therefore not an invertible representation of the waveform: for example, a signal and its sign-reversed counterpart produce identical power features, and channel-wise spectra do not preserve relative phase relationships between channels.
Spectral processing thus reduces the information available to the predictor while potentially emphasizing aspects of neural activity that are more stable or relevant to seizure suppression.
Whether this trade-off improves prediction or control depends on the dynamics that must be represented.


\subsection{Waveform Versus Spectral Prediction}
\label{sec:waveform_versus_spectral}

The stochastic drive of the biophysical plant makes exact waveform evolution uncertain over a prediction horizon.
Even if a predictor captures an oscillation's amplitude and frequency, small phase errors can produce large sample-wise errors.
Predicting spectral power avoids reproducing the individual Fourier phases and may therefore be easier.
Yet the discarded phase information may help distinguish states that evolve differently under stimulation.

Spectral prediction has its own difficulties.
Individual power estimates fluctuate even during stationary activity, which may make spectral targets harder to learn.
Averaging neighboring frames can reduce this variation, but spectral estimation over a longer measurement history, as in~\eqref{eq:stft_sample_support}, may increase detection delay.
The hop length also limits how often a new estimate is available.
These effects motivate tuning the \ac{stft} parameters for both target stability and temporal responsiveness.

For predictive control, the model must distinguish the effects of candidate stimulation currents.
Spectral power may conceal phase or amplitude responses that leave the retained power features nearly unchanged.
Whether the spectral representation still captures the responses needed for control must be tested.
The waveform--spectral comparison and the choice of \ac{stft} parameters are evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments_discussion}.
