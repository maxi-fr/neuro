\section{Regional Neural-Mass Dynamics}
\label{sec:regional_neural_mass}

Rather than tracking individual neurons, a neural mass model describes a cortical macro-column by the mean membrane potential and firing rate of whole populations.
This approximation is appropriate when the quantity of interest is a mesoscopic or macroscopic field generated by the coordinated activity of many cells~\cite{LopesDaSilva1974,Buzsaki2012}.
Such population-level dynamics are suitable for studying the regional recruitment and large-scale synchronization relevant to seizure propagation.
The Jansen-Rit model is used here because, unlike simpler phenomenological neural-mass models, it explicitly represents interacting pyramidal, excitatory interneuron and inhibitory interneuron populations with synaptic filtering, while remaining low-dimensional and capable of reproducing oscillatory and spike-wave-like epileptiform dynamics~\cite{Jansen1995,Wendling2002,Yu2024}.

Each regional node $i$ contains three interacting populations: \acp{py}, \acp{ein} and \acp{iin}, connected as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:microcircuit}.
Pyramidal firing excites both interneuron populations.
The \acp{ein} feed excitation back to the \acp{py} and therefore form a positive feedback pathway, whereas the \acp{iin} return inhibition and provide the principal negative feedback pathway.
The four intra-columnar projections are parameterized by the effective connectivity coefficients $c_1$ to $c_4$, conventionally interpreted as average synaptic contact numbers in the Jansen-Rit model.

    \centering
    \input{figures/02_biological_plant_modeling/jansen_rit_microcircuit}

    Connectivity multipliers $c_1$ to $c_4$ lie between populations, synaptic filters lie at receiving boundaries and potential summation and sigmoid conversion lie inside the receiving population.
    Black signals denote mean potentials and magenta signals denote mean firing rates.
    The two interneuron input filters share the response $x_{i,1}$: linearity allows $c_1$ and $c_3$ to precede the filters.

    Background input $I$, network input $I_{\mathrm{coup},i}$ and stochastic afferent drive $\zeta_i$ enter the excitatory synaptic pathway to the pyramidal population.
    Stimulation $s_i$ shifts the pyramidal potential before sigmoid conversion and finally, the regional output is $y_{\mathrm{reg},i}=x_{i,2}-x_{i,3}$.}
    \label{fig:microcircuit}
\end{figure}

Each synaptic pathway consists of two transformations.
First, incoming mean firing rate is converted into an average \ac{psp} by convolution with the impulse response,
%
\begin{equation}
    h_\mathrm{e}(t)=A\,a\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{-at},
    \qquad
    h_\mathrm{i}(t)=B\,b\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{-bt},
    \qquad t\geq 0\,,
    \label{eq:psp_kernel}
\end{equation}
%
where $A, B \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ are the excitatory and inhibitory synaptic gains and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ are the corresponding rate constants.
The reciprocal quantities $a^{-1}$ and $b^{-1}$ are the lumped synaptic time constants.
Then the mean membrane potential $v \in \mathbb{R}$ of a population is converted to its mean firing rate through the sigmoid function
%
\begin{equation*}
    S(v)=\frac{2e_0}{1+\exp\!\left[r(v_0-v)\right]}\,,
\end{equation*}
%
where $2e_0 \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is the maximum population firing rate, $v_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ is the potential at which half the population fires and $r \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ determines the slope of the population response reflecting the spread of firing thresholds across the cells of the population.

Because the kernels in~\eqref{eq:psp_kernel} are Green's functions of second-order linear systems, the three filtered synaptic pathways can be written as six first-order states.
The state $x_{i,1}$ is the excitatory \ac{psp} generated by pyramidal firing and delivered to the interneuron branches.
The states $x_{i,2}$ and $x_{i,3}$ are the excitatory and inhibitory \acp{psp}, respectively.
The states $x_{i,4}$, $x_{i,5}$ and $x_{i,6}$ are their corresponding time derivatives.
With node-dependent excitatory gain $A_i$, the dynamics of region $i$ are
%
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}_{i,1} &= x_{i,4}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,4} &= A_i a\,S\!\left(x_{i,2}-x_{i,3}+s_i\right)-2a\,x_{i,4}-a^2x_{i,1}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,2} &= x_{i,5}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,5} &= A_i a\left[I+c_2S\!\left(c_1x_{i,1}\right)+I_{\mathrm{coup},i}+\zeta_i(t)\right]
                     -2a\,x_{i,5}-a^2x_{i,2}, \label{eq:jr_node} \\
    \dot{x}_{i,3} &= x_{i,6}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,6} &= Bb\,c_4S\!\left(c_3x_{i,1}\right)-2b\,x_{i,6}-b^2x_{i,3}\,, \nonumber
\end{align}
%
where $I$ is the constant excitatory background drive, $I_{\mathrm{coup},i}$ is the delayed input from other regions, $s_i$ is the membrane-potential perturbation caused by external stimulation and $\zeta_i(t)$ is a zero-mean Gaussian white-noise drive with power spectral density $\sigma^2$.
The noise processes are independent across regions.

Collect the six states of~\eqref{eq:jr_node} into \mbox{$\mathbf{x}_i={\left[x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,6}\right]}^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$}.
Let $\mathbf{f}_i(\mathbf{x}_i;I_{\mathrm{coup},i},s_i)$ denote its deterministic right-hand side, obtained by setting $\zeta_i=0$.
With $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_5$ the fifth canonical basis vector in $\mathbb{R}^6$, the regional dynamics are equivalently
%
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\mathbf{x}}_i(t)
    =\mathbf{f}_i\!\left(\mathbf{x}_i(t);I_{\mathrm{coup},i}(t),s_i(t)\right)
    +A_i a\,\zeta_i(t)\hat{\mathbf{e}}_5\,.
    \label{eq:network_node}
\end{equation}
%
Stimulation shifts the membrane-potential argument of the pyramidal sigmoid in~\eqref{eq:jr_node}~\cite{Kunze2016,Merlet2013,Yu2024}.
Since $S$ increases with its argument, $s_i>0$ raises pyramidal firing and $s_i<0$ lowers it locally.

The local output of a region is the difference between the excitatory and inhibitory \acp{psp} arriving at the pyramidal population,
%
\begin{equation}
    y_{\mathrm{reg},i}(t)=x_{i,2}(t)-x_{i,3}(t)\,.
    \label{eq:regional_output}
\end{equation}
%
This difference is the net postsynaptic membrane potential of the pyramidal population, measured relative to its resting potential and expressed in millivolts.
The nodes interact through delayed firing-rate signals derived from this regional output, as defined next.

\section{Structural Coupling and Conduction Delays}
\label{sec:connectome}

The regional dynamics in~\eqref{eq:jr_node} become a whole-brain network through the input $I_{\mathrm{coup},i}$ received from other regions.
This section specifies that input from structural connection weights and transmission delays, then derives the numerical integration used to simulate the resulting stochastic delay differential system.

\subsection{Structural Coupling}

The network comprises $N=76$ brain regions, each represented by the six-state regional model defined above.
Its connection weights and tract lengths are taken from the standard structural dataset distributed with \ac{tvb}, a platform for constructing and simulating whole-brain network models~\cite{SanzLeon2013,SanzLeon2015}.
In this work, \ac{tvb} supplies the anatomical data; the numerical integration is specified separately below.

The structural connectivity matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N\times N}$ assigns a weight $w_{ij}$ to the connection from sending region $j$ to receiving region $i$.
The weights are normalized, dimensionless measures of relative connection strength rather than literal axon counts.
The present model uses symmetric weights, $w_{ij}=w_{ji}$ and excludes self-connections by setting $w_{ii}=0$.
These weights specify how strongly each sending region contributes to the receiving region's input, as in structural network models of interacting neural masses~\cite{SanzLeon2015}.

Each connection also has a tract length $\ell_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, which determines when the sending region's activity reaches the receiving region.
For a uniform conduction speed $c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, the physical transmission delay is~\cite{SanzLeon2015}
%
\begin{equation}
    d_{ij}^{\ast}=\frac{\ell_{ij}}{c}\,.
    \label{eq:physical_delay}
\end{equation}
%
Thus, longer tracts transmit activity later even when their connection weights are identical.
The chosen speed $c=\SI{50}{\milli\metre\per\milli\second}$ gives a delay of \SI{2}{\milli\second} for a \SI{100}{\milli\metre} tract.
Figure~\ref{fig:connectome_matrices} shows the connection weights and the corresponding physical delays.

\begin{figure}

    \includegraphics{figures/02_biological_plant_modeling/connectome_matrices}
    \caption{Structural connectivity weights and conduction delays for the 76-region \ac{tvb} connectome. Rows are receiving regions and columns are sending regions in atlas order. The line separates right- and left-hemisphere regions. White cells have zero connection weight; the delay panel shows only connected pairs. Delays use a uniform conduction speed of \SI{50}{\milli\metre\per\milli\second}.}
    \label{fig:connectome_matrices}
\end{figure}

The coupling transmits population firing rates rather than membrane potentials directly~\cite{SanzLeon2015,Yu2024}.
For each sending region $j$, the sigmoid $S$ converts the regional output $y_{\mathrm{reg},j}$ in~\eqref{eq:regional_output} into a firing rate.
That signal reaches region $i$ after the delay $d_{ij}^{\ast}$, is weighted by $w_{ij}$ and contributes to the sum of incoming activity.
With global coupling factor $K\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, the total input is
%
\begin{equation}
    I_{\mathrm{coup},i}(t)
    =K\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}
    S\!\left(y_{\mathrm{reg},j}(t-d_{ij}^{\ast})\right)\,.
    \label{eq:continuous_coupling}
\end{equation}
%
The factor $K$ scales all connections together and absorbs the overall normalization of the structural weights, while $w_{ij}$ retains their relative strengths.
Setting $K=0$ decouples the regions; increasing $K$ strengthens the incoming drive without changing the transmission delays.
In~\eqref{eq:jr_node}, this input enters the excitatory synaptic pathway through $\dot{x}_{i,5}$.
Activity from a connected region therefore changes the receiving region's excitatory \ac{psp}, which in turn changes its pyramidal firing through the local circuit~\cite{SanzLeon2015,Yu2024}.

Together, the regional dynamics in~\eqref{eq:network_node} and the coupling in~\eqref{eq:continuous_coupling} define the network system.
Stacking the regional states gives the current network state $\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{6N}$.
Its current value alone does not determine the network's evolution because~\eqref{eq:continuous_coupling} also depends on earlier regional outputs.
An initial history of those outputs over the longest connection delay is therefore required in addition to the initial regional states~\cite{SanzLeon2015}.

\subsection{Numerical Integration}

For simulation, the continuous-time system is approximated on a uniform time grid using a stochastic Heun scheme~\cite{SanzLeon2015}.
The plant step $\Delta t_\mathrm{p}=\SI{0.1}{\milli\second}$ gives 100 steps per \SI{10}{\milli\second} excitatory synaptic time constant. % so? why 0.1 ms?
For integration index $k\in\mathbb{N}_0$, define the grid times and numerical regional states by
%
\begin{equation}
    t_k=k\Delta t_\mathrm{p},
    \qquad \mathbf{x}_{i,k}\approx\mathbf{x}_i(t_k)\,.
\end{equation}
%
The physical delays in~\eqref{eq:physical_delay} are approximated by the nearest integer lag $n_{ij}\in\mathbb{N}_0$, giving the applied delay $d_{ij}$,
%
\begin{equation}
    n_{ij}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{d_{ij}^{\ast}}{\Delta t_\mathrm{p}}\right),
    \qquad
    d_{ij}=n_{ij}\Delta t_\mathrm{p}\,.
    \label{eq:delay_quantisation}
\end{equation}
%
Rounding changes a physical delay by at most half a plant step, here \SI{0.05}{\milli\second}.

The numerical regional output is \mbox{$y_{\mathrm{reg},j,k}=x_{j,2,k}-x_{j,3,k}$}.
Using these outputs, the continuous coupling in~\eqref{eq:continuous_coupling} is approximated at step $k$ by
%
\begin{equation*}
    I_{\mathrm{coup},i,k}
    =K\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{ij}
      S\!\left(y_{\mathrm{reg},j,k-n_{ij}}\right)\,.
\end{equation*}
%
The term $y_{\mathrm{reg},j,k-n_{ij}}$ is the sending region's output from $n_{ij}$ steps earlier.
Thus, $I_{\mathrm{coup},i,k}$ is the discrete approximation of the same network input, using the quantized delay and numerical output history.

The Gaussian white-noise drive in~\eqref{eq:network_node} acts only on the excitatory velocity state $x_{i,5}$.
Its contribution over one integration step is
%
\begin{equation*}
    \xi_{i,k}
    =A_i a\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}}\zeta_i(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
    =A_i a\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t_\mathrm{p}}\,\eta_{i,k},
    \qquad
    \text{with}\,\eta_{i,k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0, 1)\,,
\end{equation*}
%
since
%
\begin{equation*}
    \int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}}\zeta_i(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
    \sim
    \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma^2\Delta t_\mathrm{p}\right)\,.
\end{equation*}
%
The draws $\eta_{i,k}$ are independent across regions and integration steps.
With $s_{i,k}=s_i(t_k)$, the Heun predictor is
%
\begin{equation*}
    \widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i,k+1}
    =\mathbf{x}_{i,k}
    +\Delta t_\mathrm{p}\,
      \mathbf{f}_i\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{i,k};I_{\mathrm{coup},i,k},s_{i,k}\right)
      +\xi_{i,k}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_5\,,
\end{equation*}
%
and the corrected state is
%
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{x}_{i,k+1}
    ={}&\mathbf{x}_{i,k}
    +\frac{\Delta t_\mathrm{p}}{2}
      \Big[
      \mathbf{f}_i\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{i,k};I_{\mathrm{coup},i,k},s_{i,k}\right)
      +\mathbf{f}_i\!\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i,k+1};I_{\mathrm{coup},i,k},s_{i,k}\right)
      \Big]
      +\xi_{i,k}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_5\,.
\end{align*}
%
The corrector averages the deterministic drift at the current and predicted states while reusing the same noise draw~\cite{SanzLeon2015}.
The delayed coupling is approximated as constant over each plant step and the stimulation input is held constant over that step by the control implementation.
This integration advances the coupled regional dynamics; the next section specifies the regional gains $A_i$ that distinguish healthy and seizure-prone tissue.


\section{Regional Excitability and Seizure Propagation}
\label{sec:parameter_selection}

Within the whole-brain model, regional differences in seizure susceptibility are introduced through the node-dependent excitatory synaptic gain $A_i$.
Increasing $A_i$ strengthens excitation relative to inhibition and moves the isolated node toward an oscillatory instability.
For the parameter regime used here, three excitability levels are assigned following the whole-brain epilepsy configuration in~\cite{Yu2024}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
All other local Jansen-Rit parameters are shared across regions.

Healthy background regions use $A_i=3.25\,\si{\milli\volt}$ and remain in a low-amplitude stable regime when isolated.
The \ac{ez} takes $A_i = 3.6\,\si{\milli\volt}$, placing an isolated node in a self-sustained pathological oscillatory regime that does not require network recruitment.
Finally, the \ac{pz} takes an intermediate value of $A_i = 3.4\,\si{\milli\volt}$, leaving it just below its own oscillatory boundary but sufficiently excitable that coupling from an active \ac{ez} can recruit it into seizure-like activity.
Background nodes have a lower gain and are less susceptible to the same incoming drive, although sufficiently strong network activity can recruit them too.
Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation} illustrates the behaviour of nodes for these different excitability values.

Within this model seizure propagation therefore refers to network-driven recruitment of regions outside the \ac{ez}.

\subsection{Anatomical Assignment}

The assignment of the excitability levels to pathological regions follows the left mesial temporal configuration of the reference whole-brain epilepsy model in~\cite{Yu2024}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
The \ac{ez} comprises the left hippocampus, left parahippocampal cortex and left amygdala.
The \ac{pz} comprises adjacent left inferior temporal and left ventral temporal regions.
All remaining nodes are assigned the background excitability.
This configuration is intended to represent an initially mesial temporal pathological source with subsequent recruitment of nearby temporal neocortex.

\subsection{Network Calibration}
\label{sec:network_calibration}

Table~\ref{tab:jr_params} summarizes the local node parameters and network parameters used throughout the simulations.
The shared synaptic gains, time constants, sigmoid parameters, connectivity ratios and background input follow the standard Jansen-Rit parameterization~\cite{Jansen1995}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
The regional values of $A_i$ define the excitability partition described above and the conduction speed $c$ determines the tract delays through~\eqref{eq:delay_quantisation}.

The remaining network-level parameters are the global coupling factor $K$ and white-noise amplitude $\sigma$.
Increasing $K$ strengthens the delayed firing-rate input received from connected regions and therefore promotes recruitment beyond the \ac{ez}.
Increasing $\sigma$ increases stochastic variability around the deterministic trajectories and alters the timing and reliability of recruitment in regions close to their oscillatory boundary.
The pair $(K,\sigma)$ was calibrated to obtain an uncontrolled propagation pattern qualitatively resembling the left-temporal whole-brain epilepsy simulation in~\cite{Yu2024}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
There, recruitment spreads through most of the left hemisphere while not extending to the right hemisphere~\cite{Yu2024}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
Inspired by this sequence, the present study targets recruitment through the \ac{pz} across the left hemisphere within approximately \SI{10}{\second} of \ac{ez} seizure onset.
The resulting values are $K=0.60$ and $\sigma=0.86\,\si{\second^{-1/2}}$.
A region is classified as recruited if within the past \SI{1}{\second} the peak-to-peak amplitude of $y_{\mathrm{reg}}$ exceeds \SI{5}{\milli\volt}.
This criterion is used consistently for the calibration and subsequent propagation analyses, with its detailed motivation and sensitivity reported in the appendix.
The exact spread pattern remains stochastic across random seeds.
Figure~\ref{fig:uncontrolled_propagation} shows a representative run with near-full left-hemisphere recruitment after approximately \SI{10}{\second}.

The preceding sections define the autonomous and network-driven evolution of the neural state.
Closed-loop control additionally requires an observation map from this hidden state to measurable scalp signals and an actuation map from electrode currents back to the neural dynamics.

\section{Electrical Interfaces}
\label{sec:electrical_interfaces}
The two maps describe volume conduction through the tissues of the head: from neural sources to scalp potentials for measurement and from electrode currents to intracranial electric fields for stimulation~\cite{SanzLeon2015,Huang2019}.
Under the quasi-static approximation and fixed tissue conductivities, electrical potentials and fields depend linearly on their sources~\cite{SanzLeon2015,Huang2019}.
The resulting lead-field matrices encode the spatial distribution of these potentials and fields for the chosen head models and electrode configurations.

\subsection{Scalp \acs{eeg} Measurement}
\label{sec:eeg_measurement}

Scalp \ac{eeg} provides the observation channel for the controller.
Biophysically, synaptic currents flowing along the approximately aligned somato-dendritic axes of cortical \acp{py}, together with their return currents, form equivalent current dipoles whose dominant orientation is approximately normal to the local cortical surface.
Because neighboring pyramidal neurons have similar orientations, their contributions can sum coherently and dominate the extracellular field at macroscopic scales~\cite{Buzsaki2012}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
The Jansen-Rit output $y_{\mathrm{reg},i}$ in~\eqref{eq:regional_output} is the net excitatory-minus-inhibitory postsynaptic membrane potential of the pyramidal population, not a calibrated current-dipole moment.
A fixed proportionality between this output and regional source density is assumed, as is customary when using neural-mass outputs for \ac{eeg} forward modeling~\cite{Jansen1995,David2003}.
Volume conduction through the brain, skull and scalp then maps these sources to \ac{eeg} measurements at the electrodes.
With fixed geometry and conductivities, the regional contributions superpose linearly~\cite{SanzLeon2015} as
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{y}_\mathrm{EEG}(t)
    =\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG}\,\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{reg}(t)\,.
\end{equation*}
%
Here $\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{reg}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ collects the regional outputs, $\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{EEG}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$ contains the sensor measurements and $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ is the regional \ac{eeg} lead field.

The \ac{tvb} surface projection has $M=62$ sensor rows and 16,384 cortical-vertex columns~\cite{SanzLeon2015}.
If $V_i$ is the set of vertices assigned to region $i$ and $L^{\mathrm{surf}}_{mv}$ is the projection from vertex $v$ to channel $m$, the regional matrix is
%
\begin{equation*}
    [\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG}]_{mi}=\sum_{v\in V_i}L^{\mathrm{surf}}_{mv}\,.
\end{equation*}
%
Thus, all vertices in a region share its neural-mass time course, while the surface projection supplies their different sensor weights.
The summation corresponds to treating $y_{\mathrm{reg},i}$ as a spatially uniform source-density amplitude over the vertices assigned to region $i$. Consequently, larger represented cortical regions can contribute through a larger surface extent.
This aggregation gives the $62\times 76$ matrix used in the plant.
The reference convention of the supplied \ac{tvb} projection is retained in $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG}$.
Because the assumed proportionality between neural-mass output and physical dipole density is not calibrated, the resulting simulated \ac{eeg} is a linearly scaled observation signal rather than an absolute prediction in clinical \si{\micro\volt}.

\subsection{\acl{tes}}
\label{sec:stimulation_interface}

The actuation proceeds in the opposite physical direction.
In \ac{tes}, currents applied at scalp electrodes generate a distributed electric field inside the head.
The pyramidal populations are modeled as being polarized by the component of this field along their somato-dendritic axis.
At the regional scale used here, this axis is approximated by the local cortical normal, so the normal electric-field component determines the effective membrane-potential perturbation~\cite{Buzsaki2012}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
%At the regional scale used here, that axis is approximated by the regional orientation vector supplied with the \ac{tvb} connectivity and treated as a cortical-normal surrogate, so the field component along this orientation determines the effective membrane-potential perturbation~\cite{Buzsaki2012}. % note that for stimulation not TVB but ROAST is used
%For mesial and non-neocortical regions such as the hippocampus and amygdala, this orientation is only a coarse modeling surrogate for the local somato-dendritic axis; the resulting stimulation sensitivity should therefore be interpreted at the regional-model level rather than as a detailed cellular polarization estimate.
This motivates the additive perturbation $s_i$ in the argument of the pyramidal sigmoid in~\eqref{eq:jr_node}, rather than introducing stimulation as an additional synaptic firing-rate input~\cite{Kunze2016,Merlet2013,Yu2024}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED

Electric-field basis solutions were computed with ROAST, an automated finite-element modeling pipeline for \ac{tes}, using its standard MNI152 head model~\cite{Huang2019}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
The selected montage comprises $P=3$ electrodes, ordered as TP9, CP5 and Ex8 in the current vector.
Ex8 lies on the right posterior side of the lower head and belongs to ROAST's extension of the standard 10/05 electrode layout.~\cite{ROASTElectrodeLayout}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
It serves here as the reference/return electrode, following its use to limit the influence of the return electrode on the modeled brain regions in~\cite{Yu2024}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
For each non-reference electrode $p$, let $\mathbf{E}_{p,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ denote the electric-field basis response per milliampere at the centroid of region $i$.
It is computed by injecting \SI{1}{\milli\ampere} at electrode $p$ with the return current flowing through Ex8.
The unit vector $\mathbf{n}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ specifies the regional orientation used to project this field onto the somato-dendritic axis.
The assumed polarization length $\lambda_\mathrm{p}=\SI{0.35}{\milli\metre}$ converts the projected field into a membrane-potential shift~\cite{Radman2009}.
The resulting stimulation lead field $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times P}$ contains the regional membrane-potential shifts per milliampere of electrode current.
Let $\mathbf{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{P}$ collect the electrode currents and $\mathbf{s}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ the resulting regional stimulation drives.
With currents expressed in milliamperes and stimulation drives in millivolts, their relation is
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{s}(t)=\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}\mathbf{u}(t)\,,
\end{equation*}
%
with regional entries
%
\begin{equation*}
    s_i(t)=\sum_{p=1}^{P}[\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}]_{ip}u_p(t),
    \qquad
    [\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}]_{ip}
    =\lambda_\mathrm{p}\,\mathbf{E}_{p,i}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{n}_i\,.
\end{equation*}
%
Ex8 is retained as the third entry of $\mathbf{u}$ so that the physical electrode currents satisfy Kirchhoff's current law.
As the reference/return electrode used to construct the FEM basis, it has a zero column in $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}$.

The montage was selected by a three-electrode search over the available scalp locations for targeted suppressive drive in the \ac{pz}.
The search procedure and montage comparison are reported in the appendix.
By design, the individual electrode currents are constrained to respect safety and hardware limits,
%
\begin{equation*}
    |u_p(t)|\leq\SI{2}{\milli\ampere},
    \qquad p=1,\ldots,P\,,
\end{equation*}
%
while charge conservation across the montage requires
%
\begin{equation*}
    \sum_{p=1}^{P}u_p(t)=0\,,
\end{equation*}
%
so that the applied montage injects no net current into the head.
Although sensing and stimulation are both governed by volume conduction, the two lead fields used here are not transposes of one another.
This difference does not violate Helmholtz reciprocity.
Helmholtz reciprocity relates measurement and stimulation configurations only when source geometry, electrode pairs and referencing are matched~\cite{Malmivuo1995}. % cite-checked: SUPPORTED
Here, the main difference is that Ex8 is used in the stimulation montage but is absent from the 62-channel \ac{tvb} \ac{eeg} layout.

This completes the specification of the biophysical plant. Chapter~\ref{ch:data_driven_predictor_design} next considers how its scalp measurements can be used to construct predictive models for closed-loop control.
