\chapter{Problem Formulation}\label{ch:problem_formulation}

This chapter establishes the biological foundations and control engineering setting of the thesis.
It introduces neural activity and its representation across scales, explains the recruitment of brain regions during focal seizures and describes the available measurement and stimulation interfaces.
The final section places these elements in the simulation and feedback control setting used in this work.

\section{Neural Activity and Modeling Across Scales}\label{sec:neural_activity_background}

Neural activity comprises the electrical signaling of individual neurons and the collective responses of their interacting populations.
A neuron maintains an electrical potential difference across its cell membrane.
Changes in ionic currents across that membrane alter the membrane potential and can generate an action potential, a brief electrical pulse that travels along the axon~\cite{Hodgkin1952}.
Neurons communicate with one another through synapses, specialized junctions between cells~\cite{Sudhof2021}.
At a chemical synapse, activity in the sending neuron changes the input received by the receiving neuron.
Excitatory inputs tend to promote firing, whereas inhibitory inputs tend to restrain it~\cite{Wilson1972}.
At this cellular scale, conductance-based descriptions such as the Hodgkin-Huxley model describe how ionic currents generate these signals~\cite{Hodgkin1952}.

Neurons participate in circuits with recurrent excitation and inhibition.
Their collective activity can exhibit oscillations even though individual neurons need not fire on every oscillation cycle~\cite{Buzsaki2012}.
Neural mass models describe these collective dynamics through population quantities such as mean firing rate and mean postsynaptic potential~\cite{SanzLeon2013}.
A postsynaptic potential is the membrane-potential response to synaptic input.
Population descriptions retain the interactions between groups of neurons while leaving individual spike timings unresolved.
Wilson-Cowan models describe interacting excitatory and inhibitory population activity, while Jansen-Rit models include synaptic filtering and population membrane potentials~\cite{Wilson1972,Jansen1995}.

In cortical neural mass models, pyramidal cells form an important excitatory population that interacts with local interneurons~\cite{Jansen1995}.
A cortical macro-column is represented as a local circuit of such populations extending through the layers of the cortex, the folded outer layer of the cerebrum.
Using a neural mass model to describe an entire brain region is a further coarse-graining: its variables represent regional activity aggregated over many local circuits~\cite{SanzLeon2013,SanzLeon2015}.
Figure~\ref{fig:neural_scales} illustrates this relationship between regional network nodes, interacting populations and their constituent neurons.

At the whole-brain scale, regions communicate through bundles of axons, including long-range white-matter pathways~\cite{Hagmann2008}.
Whole-brain network models connect regional models through a structural connectome, a representation of anatomical connections between brain regions~\cite{SanzLeon2013}.
Constructing this connectome involves a parcellation that divides the brain into anatomical units and an estimate of the pathways connecting them.
Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging measures direction-dependent water diffusion in tissue, from which local fiber orientations can be estimated.
Tractography follows these estimated orientations to reconstruct candidate white-matter pathways. % PROSE-11?
Combining these pathways with the parcellation yields regional connection weights and estimates of pathway lengths~\cite{Hagmann2008}.

In a dynamical network, connection weights determine the strength of interactions, while pathway lengths and conduction speeds determine transmission delays~\cite{SanzLeon2015}.
Such networks have been used to investigate how regional excitability and anatomical connectivity shape seizure recruitment~\cite{Olmi2019}.

\section{Focal Seizures and Recruitment}\label{sec:seizure_recruitment_background}

The regional interactions described above provide the pathways for pathological activity to spread.
An epileptic seizure is a temporary episode of clinical signs or symptoms arising from abnormally excessive or synchronized neuronal activity~\cite{Fisher2005}.
The term focal describes seizures that originate within networks limited to one hemisphere; the activity may subsequently spread beyond its initial territory.
The resulting manifestations depend on the neural systems involved~\cite{Fisher2017}.

For the network description adopted here, the \ac{ez} denotes the regions that initiate seizure activity.
Recruitment means that a region outside the initiating zone develops seizure-like activity through its interaction with the active network.
It is a change in that region's dynamics, rather than merely the detection of an electrical signal generated elsewhere~\cite{Olmi2019}. % what is "it"? the version before was clearer

Whether a region is recruited depends on both its local susceptibility and the input it receives from other regions.
Structural connectivity alone therefore does not determine the propagation pattern~\cite{Olmi2019}.
Population models can represent differences in susceptibility through regional parameters controlling the balance of excitation and inhibition~\cite{Wendling2002,Yu2024}.

This thesis investigates whether seizure activity can be suppressed by using electrical stimulation to influence these regional dynamics.
The primary control objective is full suppression of seizure activity within the \ac{ez} and prevention of recruitment outside it.
A secondary objective is to limit stimulation effort by reducing electrode-current magnitudes and the number of intervals in which stimulation is applied.

\section{Measurement and Stimulation}\label{sec:measurement_intervention_background}

Suppressing seizure activity requires a way to observe neural activity and an intervention that can influence its evolution.
\Ac{eeg} records electrical potential differences between electrodes on the scalp.
The recorded potentials reflect the superposition of contributions from neural currents after conduction through the tissues of the head.
Consequently, an electrode does not directly measure the activity of a single underlying region.
The relationship between regional seizure activity and the available measurements is therefore indirect~\cite{Buzsaki2012}.

In \ac{tes}, electrical currents are applied through scalp electrodes to influence neural activity.
These currents produce electric fields within the head, with their spatial distribution depending on the electrode arrangement and the electrical properties of the tissues~\cite{Huang2019}.
The effects of stimulation on collective neural activity can be investigated by combining a head model with a model of neural populations~\cite{Merlet2013,Kunze2016}.
Together, scalp measurements and stimulation provide the observation and intervention interfaces for feedback control.

\section{Simulation and \ac{mpc} Framework}\label{sec:control_engineering_setting}

The scalp measurement and stimulation interfaces define the feedback control setting investigated here in simulation.
The biophysical plant model represents a network of regional neural masses coupled according to anatomical connectivity, with mappings that convert electrode currents into regional inputs and regional activity into scalp \ac{eeg} potentials.
Although regional activity is available for evaluating seizure suppression, the controller has access only to scalp \ac{eeg} and the applied electrode currents.
Figure~\ref{fig:control_setting} provides an overview of the feedback loop and the offline identification procedure described below.

\Ac{mpc} uses a dynamical model of the brain network to predict its response to candidate sequences of electrode currents.
A cost function assesses the predicted response and stimulation effort, and the current sequence is optimized subject to constraints.
Only the first input is applied before the optimization is repeated as new measurements become available~\cite{Rawlings2017}.

For prediction, the controller uses an identified model rather than the plant's mechanistic equations or internal neural states.
Before closed-loop evaluation, plant simulations generate datasets of applied currents and resulting \ac{eeg} measurements for offline system identification~\cite{Ljung1999}.
The identified model uses histories of measurements and currents to forecast the response to candidate stimulation sequences over a finite horizon.
Depending on the prediction target, it forecasts either \ac{eeg} waveforms or spectral power derived from the \ac{stft}.

Because regional neural activity is unavailable to the controller, the cost expresses seizure suppression through deviations of predicted scalp activity from a healthy reference.
Waveform tracking penalizes deviations from healthy channel means, whereas spectral suppression penalizes power above a healthy reference envelope for each channel and frequency.
The spectral cost can be evaluated directly from predicted spectral power or from spectra computed from predicted waveforms.
Additional cost terms penalize electrode-current magnitudes or the number of intervals in which stimulation is applied, balancing seizure suppression against stimulation effort.

In addition to the cost, constraints restrict which stimulation sequences are admissible.
Electrode-current bounds limit the magnitude of each current, while a current-balance constraint requires the electrode currents to sum to zero at each stimulation interval.

Both identification and control evaluation therefore depend on a plant that represents seizure recruitment and its response to scalp stimulation.
The next chapter specifies the regional neural dynamics, network coupling and scalp mappings that constitute this plant.
