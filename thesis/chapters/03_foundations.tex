\section{Whole-Brain Biophysical Modeling}

The controllers designed in this work need to be evaluated through simulations against a ground thruth model plant.
The plant used throughout this work is a whole-brain network of Jansen-Rit neural masses coupled
through a structural connectome, following the epilepsy model of Yu et al.~\cite{Yu2024}.
This section develops that model from the single cortical column to the network, and closes with the two electrical interfaces the controller acts through: the scalp \ac{eeg} it measures and the stimulation drive it applies.

\subsection{The Jansen-Rit Neural Mass Model}

A neural mass model abandons the individual neuron and describes a cortical macro-column
by the mean membrane potential and mean firing rate of whole populations, an approximation
justified when the quantity of interest --- the extracellular field --- is itself a % no em dash
spatial average over millions of cells~\cite{LopesDaSilva1974,Nunez2006}. Lopes da Silva
et al.\ introduced the formalism to explain the thalamic alpha rhythm~\cite{LopesDaSilva1974},
and Jansen and Rit gave it the three-population form used here, which reproduces both
resting alpha activity and visual evoked potentials from one parameter set~\cite{Jansen1995}.
Its appeal for this work is the combination of physiological
interpretability as well as the ability to produce waveforms characteristic of epileptic seizures~\cite{Wendling2002,Yu2024}.
This differentiates it from simpler models such as Wilson-Cowan or FitzHugh-Nagumo, both only two instead of six states, separate excitation from inhibition but collapse the synaptic filtering setting the frequency of the resulting
rhythm, and produce oscillations without the spike-wave morphology making a simulated
seizure recognisable~\cite{David2003}.

Each node $i$ comprises a population of \acp{py} that projects to, and receives
feedback from, a population of \acp{ein} and a population of \acp{iin}, wired as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:microcircuit}.
The excitatory branch forms a positive feedback loop and the inhibitory
branch a negative one, and the competition between the two produces the oscillations the
model is built to explain. The strengths of the four intra-columnar projections are the
average synaptic contact numbers $c_1$ to $c_4$, held at the standard proportions
$c_2 = 0.8\,c_1$ and $c_3 = c_4 = 0.25\,c_1$ so that a single number $c_1$ scales the
whole microcircuit~\cite{Jansen1995}.

    $I_{\mathrm{coup},i}$ and the noise $\zeta_i$ enter the excitatory branch, the
    stimulation drive $s_i$ enters at the pyramidal soma, and the node output
    $y_{\mathrm{LFP},i} = x_{i,2} - x_{i,3}$ is tapped there.}
    \label{fig:microcircuit}

Every population converts its input in two stages. The first is a linear pulse-to-wave
operator turning an incoming mean firing rate into an average \ac{psp} by convolution
with the impulse response
%
\begin{equation}
    h_\mathrm{e}(t) = A\,a\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{-a t}, \qquad
    h_\mathrm{i}(t) = B\,b\,t\,\mathrm{e}^{-b t}, \qquad t \geq 0 \,,
    \label{eq:psp_kernel}
\end{equation}
%
in which the synaptic gains $A$ and $B$ set the amplitude of the excitatory and inhibitory
\ac{psp} in \si{\milli\volt} and the rate constants $a$ and $b$ set the reciprocal
lumped time constants of dendritic delay and membrane decay. The second stage is a static
wave-to-pulse operator mapping the mean membrane potential $v$ of a population onto its
mean firing rate through the sigmoid
%
\begin{equation}
    S(v) = \frac{2 e_0}{1 + \exp\!\big(r (v_0 - v)\big)} \,,
    \label{eq:sigmoid}
\end{equation}
%
whose ceiling $2 e_0$ is the maximum population firing rate, whose midpoint $v_0$ is the
potential at which half the population fires, and whose steepness $r$ reflects the spread of
firing thresholds across the cells of the population.

Because the kernel in~\eqref{eq:psp_kernel} is the Green's function of a second-order linear
operator, the convolution can be written as a pair of first-order differential equations, and
the three populations together give the six states $x_{i,1}$ to $x_{i,6}$ per node. The
potentials $x_{i,1}$, $x_{i,2}$ and $x_{i,3}$ are the \ac{psp} of the pyramidal, excitatory and
inhibitory branch, and $x_{i,4}$, $x_{i,5}$ and $x_{i,6}$ are their time derivatives, so that
the node obeys
%
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}_{i,1} &= x_{i,4}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,4} &= A_i a\, S\!\left(x_{i,2} - x_{i,3} + s_i\right) - 2 a\, x_{i,4} - a^2 x_{i,1}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,2} &= x_{i,5}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,5} &= A_i a \left[ I + c_2 S\!\left(c_1 x_{i,1}\right) + I_{\mathrm{coup},i} \right] - 2 a\, x_{i,5} - a^2 x_{i,2} + \zeta_i, \label{eq:jr_node} \\
    \dot{x}_{i,3} &= x_{i,6}, \nonumber \\
    \dot{x}_{i,6} &= B b\, c_4 S\!\left(c_3 x_{i,1}\right) - 2 b\, x_{i,6} - b^2 x_{i,3} \,, \nonumber
\end{align}
%
where $I$ is the constant excitatory background input standing in for afferents the model does
not resolve, $I_{\mathrm{coup},i}$ the input from the rest of the network developed in
Section~\ref{sec:connectome}, and $s_i$ the stimulation drive of Section~\ref{sec:electro}.
The term $\zeta_i$ is Gaussian white noise of intensity $\sigma$ entering the excitatory loop.
The noise is a necessary component. A node sitting just below its bifurcation has two
coexisting attractors, and only the noise pushes it from the quiet one into the
oscillating one, so the noise is the mechanism by which
seizures start at irregular times and by which repeated runs of one configuration differ~\cite{Yu2024}. Table~\ref{tab:jr_params} collects the parameter values, which are those of the
original single-column fit~\cite{Jansen1995} except where the network model of Yu et al.\
prescribes otherwise~\cite{Yu2024}.

The quantity read out of a node is the net depolarisation of the pyramidal population,
%
\begin{equation}
    y_{\mathrm{LFP},i}(t) = x_{i,2}(t) - x_{i,3}(t) \,,
    \label{eq:lfp}
\end{equation}
%
the difference between the excitatory and inhibitory \ac{psp} arriving at the pyramidal
soma. Its physical interpretation is taken up in Section~\ref{sec:electro}; for the dynamics
it matters that $y_{\mathrm{LFP},i}$ is also the argument of the pyramidal sigmoid in~\eqref{eq:jr_node}, so the output of a node and its own feedback are the same signal.

\subsection{Regional Excitability and Seizure Onset}
\label{sec:excitability}

The excitatory synaptic gain $A_i$ is the parameter deciding which regime a node
settles into, which is why it carries a regional index while every other
parameter in~\eqref{eq:jr_node} is shared. Increasing $A_i$ strengthens the excitatory
feedback loop relative to the inhibitory one, and the node passes through a Hopf
bifurcation from a stable fixed point, around which noise produces low-amplitude alpha-band
fluctuations, to a large-amplitude limit cycle of spike-wave morphology. Following the
excitability partition established for whole-brain epilepsy models~\cite{Yu2024}, three levels are used. Healthy background tissue takes
$A_i = 3.25\,\si{\milli\volt}$ and rests at the fixed point. The \ac{ez} takes
$A_i = 3.6\,\si{\milli\volt}$ and oscillates autonomously, needing no input from the
network to seize. The \ac{pz} takes the intermediate $A_i = 3.4\,\si{\milli\volt}$, which
leaves it below its own bifurcation but close enough that coupling from an active \ac{ez}
recruits it.

\subsection{Connectome Coupling and Conduction Delays}
\label{sec:connectome}

Scaling from one column to a brain means specifying which nodes talk to which, how
strongly, and how long the signal takes to arrive. The first two are carried by the
structural connectivity matrix $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ over $N$ regions,
whose entry $w_{ij}$ is the density of fibres between regions $i$ and $j$; the matrix is
symmetric, since diffusion-weighted tractography and the tracer collations it is validated
against recover fibre bundles without their direction~\cite{Kotter2004,SanzLeon2013}. A
single global coupling factor $K$ scales the whole matrix and is the one free network
parameter, $\mathbf{W}$ itself being measured rather than fitted.

Long-range cortico-cortical projections are axonal, so what one region transmits to another
is a firing rate rather than a potential, and it arrives late. The delay follows from the
fibre length $\ell_{ij}$ between the two regions and a conduction speed $c$ assumed uniform
across the white matter, giving the delay matrix $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$
with entries $d_{ij} = \ell_{ij} / c$. Delayed presynaptic firing enters the excitatory
\ac{psp} channel of the receiving node, so the coupling term of~\eqref{eq:jr_node} is
%
\begin{equation}
    I_{\mathrm{coup},i}(t) = K \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{ij}\, S\!\big(y_{\mathrm{LFP},j}(t - d_{ij})\big) \,.
    \label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
%
Together,~\eqref{eq:jr_node} and~\eqref{eq:coupling} constitute a stochastic delay
differential equation in the $6N$-dimensional state
$\mathbf{x}(t) = \big[x_{1, 1}(t), \dots, x_{N, 6}(t)\big]^\top$, whose solution from a given
initial history is the plant every later chapter treats as ground truth.

With $\ell_{ij}$ on the order of \SI{100}{\milli\metre} and $c$ on the order of
\SI{50}{\milli\metre\per\milli\second}, inter-regional delays reach several milliseconds and
are comparable to the membrane time constants in Table~\ref{tab:jr_params}, so they shape
the phase relations deciding whether an active \ac{ez} recruits a neighbour.

\subsection{Electrophysiological Interpretation}
\label{sec:electro}

So far $y_{\mathrm{LFP},i}$ has been a state combination. Its physical reading is that
synaptic currents entering the apical dendrites of a pyramidal population, together with
the return currents at the soma, form a current dipole oriented along the somato-dendritic
axis of the cell; because pyramidal cells are aligned perpendicular to the cortical
sheet, these dipoles sum coherently across a region instead of cancelling, and they, not
the action potentials, dominate the extracellular field~\cite{Nunez2006}. The net somatic depolarisation $x_{i,2} - x_{i,3}$ is the only variable of the model tracking
that current balance, and it is taken as a proxy for the regional dipole moment up to an
undetermined constant of proportionality, the convention throughout neural mass modelling
of \ac{eeg}~\cite{David2003,SanzLeon2015}. The convention is a modelling choice rather
than a derivation: the model carries no cortical geometry, so it fixes neither the sign
nor the scale of the dipole, and~\eqref{eq:lfp} therefore predicts the waveform of the
regional \ac{lfp} but not its absolute amplitude.

Scalp \ac{eeg} follows from the \ac{lfp} by volume conduction through the head. Quasi-static
Maxwell equations make the map linear, so with
$\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{LFP}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ collecting the regional \acp{lfp} the $M$
channel voltages are
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}_\mathrm{EEG}(t) = \mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG}\, \mathbf{y}_\mathrm{LFP}(t) \,,
    \label{eq:eeg}
\end{equation}
%
where the \ac{eeg} lead field $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N}$ holds in
entry $(m,i)$ the voltage electrode $m$ records per unit dipole moment in region $i$~\cite{SanzLeon2015}. The dipole scale being undetermined, $\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{EEG}$ carries
the arbitrary amplitude units of $\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{LFP}$ rather than volts, which is why
every threshold and cost in the later chapters is stated relative to the background level
of the model itself.

Stimulation runs in the opposite direction. Transcranial currents injected at $P$ scalp
electrodes produce an electric field in the head, and the component of that field along the
somato-dendritic axis polarises the pyramidal membrane, shifting its operating point on the
sigmoid without injecting a firing rate of its own. The perturbation therefore enters~\eqref{eq:jr_node} inside the pyramidal sigmoid as the additive drive $s_i$, the
macroscopic $\lambda_\mathrm{E}$ formulation of \ac{tes} adopted by large-scale modelling
studies~\cite{Kunze2016,Merlet2013,Yu2024}. Collecting the electrode currents in
$\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^{P}$ and the regional drives in $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$,
the drive is
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s}(t) = \mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}\, \mathbf{u}(t), \qquad
    \mathbf{1}^\top \mathbf{u}(t) = 0 \,,
    \label{eq:stim}
\end{equation}
%
with the stimulation lead field $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times P}$ and
the zero-sum constraint imposed by Kirchhoff's current law, which forbids current from accumulating in the head.

The two lead fields in~\eqref{eq:eeg} and~\eqref{eq:stim} describe the same physics viewed
from opposite ends. Helmholtz reciprocity states that the lead field relating a source in
the brain to a pair of scalp electrodes is identical to the field the same electrode pair
would produce in the brain when driven by unit current~\cite{Malmivuo1995}, so
$\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{stim}$ and $\mathbf{L}_\mathrm{EEG}^\top$ ought to agree up to scaling.
How the two lead fields are obtained in practice is taken up in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

The plant is now fully specified, and with it the difficulty the rest of the thesis
addresses. The controller must regulate a $6N$-dimensional delayed stochastic system while
observing only $M$ linear mixtures of it and acting only through $P$ scalp currents, and it
must do so fast enough to intervene before recruitment spreads. Those $M$ mixtures reach the
controller as raw voltage traces, and the following section develops the spectral estimate
that turns them into the quantity the loop acts on. Section~\ref{sec:mpc} then develops the control formulation, and states in
doing so what it demands of a prediction model; Section~\ref{sec:sysid} turns to identifying
such a model from data, since solving the plant equations online is out of reach.

\section{The Short-Time Fourier Transform}
\label{sec:stft}

Reading \ac{eeg} through its power spectrum is standard practice, and
Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation} already did so: the seizing nodes there carry some three orders of
magnitude more power below \SI{10}{\hertz} than the background node and roll off steeply above
\SI{20}{\hertz}, where the background spectrum decays gradually across the whole band. The
estimate behind that figure averages over the whole four-second record, so it reports one
number per frequency for an interval during which the network may have changed regime several
times. Recovering the time axis means computing the same kind of estimate from a short
trailing stretch of signal and refreshing it as the signal arrives. The \ac{stft} is that
construction. It is used throughout the predictor and controller design of this thesis, and
this section develops it once, from the segmentation of the sampled signal to the averaged
spectra the later chapters work with.

Only one channel is treated here. Every operation below acts on each channel of
$\mathbf{y}_\mathrm{EEG}$ separately, so the channel index is dropped throughout the section
and restored where the channels are stacked again.

\subsection{Segmentation and the Time-Frequency Trade-Off}
\label{sec:stft_grid}

The plant evolves in continuous time, while everything downstream of the electrodes reads it
on a sampling grid. Fixing a sampling interval $\Delta t = 1 /
f_\mathrm{s}$ and writing $t_k = k \, \Delta t$, the sampled channel is
%
\begin{equation*}
    y_k = y_\mathrm{EEG}(t_k), \qquad k \in \mathbb{Z} \,,
\end{equation*}
%
and $k$ indexes both the measurements and, from Section~\ref{sec:mpc} on, the steps at which
the controller acts. The \ac{stft} cuts that stream into segments of $L_\mathrm{s}$ samples
spaced $H_\mathrm{s}$ samples apart, multiplies each segment by a taper $g_n$, and transforms
it. Segment $q$ starts at sample $q H_\mathrm{s}$, so with $n$ counting samples from the start
of a segment its coefficients are
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_q(f_\nu) = \sum_{n=0}^{L_\mathrm{s}-1} g_n \, y_{q H_\mathrm{s} + n}
    \, \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i} 2 \pi \nu n / L_\mathrm{s}},
    \qquad f_\nu = \nu \, f_\mathrm{s} / L_\mathrm{s} \,,
    \label{eq:stft}
\end{equation}
%
where $\nu = 0, \dots, L_\mathrm{s}/2$ indexes the frequency bins of the real-input transform
and $f_\nu$ is the frequency each bin sits at. The segment length $L_\mathrm{s}$ and the hop
$H_\mathrm{s}$ are the two integers that fix the grid, and Figure~\ref{fig:stft_geometry}
draws it.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/03_foundations/stft_geometry}
    \caption{The grid an \ac{stft} imposes, drawn on a linear chirp sweeping
    \SIrange{4}{45}{\hertz} over \SI{3}{\second} at $f_\mathrm{s} = \SI{200}{\hertz}$. Top,

    needs. The arrow marks the $L_\mathrm{s}$ samples the first spectrum waits for.}
    \label{fig:stft_geometry}
\end{figure}

The segment length settles two things at once. Segment $q$ ends at sample
$q H_\mathrm{s} + L_\mathrm{s} - 1$ and uses no sample after it, so the transform runs online,
though not before that sample has arrived: each spectrum lags the oldest sample it carries by
$L_\mathrm{s} \, \Delta t$ seconds, and anything acting on those spectra inherits the lag. The
same length also fixes the resolution. Bins are spaced $\Delta f = f_\mathrm{s}/L_\mathrm{s}$
apart, which is also the width of the central peak the finite segment convolves the true
spectrum with, so two components closer together than $\Delta f$ are reported as one~\cite{Oppenheim2010}. Resolving finer structure means a longer segment, and a longer segment
means a longer delay.

The taper $g_n$ is the remaining freedom. Taking a segment without one multiplies the signal
by a rectangular window, whose transform has ripples flanking the central peak, the sidelobes,
decaying only as $1/f$. A strong low-frequency component then leaks power across the whole
band and buries weaker structure. Tapering the segment edges smoothly to zero suppresses the
sidelobes and widens the central peak to roughly twice $\Delta f$ in exchange~\cite{Oppenheim2010}. This thesis uses the periodic Hann taper throughout, in the training
loss and in the controller alike.

\subsection{Power Estimation and Averaging}
\label{sec:stft_power}

Only the magnitude of~\eqref{eq:stft} is carried further. The phase records where within a
segment each component sits, and that shifts with every hop even when the spectrum itself does
not move, whereas what separates the regimes of Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation} is how much power
sits where. Each segment is therefore reduced to the one-sided power spectral density
%
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Phi}_q(f_\nu) = \frac{\alpha_\nu \, |Y_q(f_\nu)|^2}
    {f_\mathrm{s} \sum_{n=0}^{L_\mathrm{s}-1} g_n^2},
    \qquad
    \alpha_\nu =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \nu \in \{0, \, L_\mathrm{s}/2\}, \\
        2, & \text{otherwise},
    \end{cases} \,,
    \label{eq:periodogram}
\end{equation}
%
the periodogram of segment $q$. The factor $\alpha_\nu$ folds the negative frequencies onto
the positive ones, sparing the two bins that have no partner. The denominator holds the taper
energy $\sum_n g_n^2$, which the Hann definition does not normalise to one, and dividing it
out makes the result a density per hertz whose value does not depend on how long the segment
was or on how much the taper attenuated it~\cite{Oppenheim2010}. Without that normalisation a
comparison between two segment lengths would confound a change of resolution with a change of
scale.

Equation~\eqref{eq:periodogram} is a poor estimator, and poor in a way lengthening the segment
does not repair. For a stationary Gaussian process the real and imaginary parts of $Y_q(f_\nu)$
are asymptotically independent and Gaussian with variance proportional to the true density
$\Phi(f_\nu)$, so $\hat{\Phi}_q$ is that density scaled by a chi-squared variable with two
degrees of freedom~\cite{Oppenheim2010}. Its standard deviation therefore equals its mean,
whatever $L_\mathrm{s}$ is. Doubling the segment doubles the number of bins and leaves the
scatter within each of them untouched, which is why a single periodogram of a long record
looks like noise drawn around the shape it is meant to reveal. Averaging is the only remedy.
The mean of $L_\mathrm{k}$ periodograms of independent segments has $2 L_\mathrm{k}$ degrees
of freedom and $L_\mathrm{k}$ times less variance, at no cost in resolution. Averaging the
periodograms of every segment in a record is Welch's method, and it is what produced the
spectra of Figure~\ref{fig:bifurcation}.

Welch's average over a whole record returns a single spectrum and so gives up the time axis
again. Averaging over a trailing group of segments instead keeps it,
%
\begin{equation}
    \bar{\Phi}_q(f_\nu) = \sum_{q'=0}^{L_\mathrm{k}-1} \kappa_{q'} \,
    \hat{\Phi}_{q-q'}(f_\nu) \,,
    \label{eq:frame_kernel}
\end{equation}
%
with non-negative weights $\kappa_{q'}$ summing to one over a width of $L_\mathrm{k}$
segments. Uniform weights make~\eqref{eq:frame_kernel} a Welch estimate over the trailing
$L_\mathrm{k}$ segments, recomputed at every hop. A triangular or Hann weighting instead
discounts older segments, which is the sensible choice when the spectrum is expected to move.
The kernel costs a further $(L_\mathrm{k} - 1) H_\mathrm{s}$ samples of delay and leaves
$\Delta f$ untouched, so it buys degrees of freedom rather than resolution and is a knob
separate from the segment length. Figure~\ref{fig:stft_resolution} separates the two effects.

\section{Model Predictive Control}
\label{sec:mpc}

The control problem in this work combines a performance criterion for seizure suppression
with hard constraints on the electrode currents. \Ac{mpc} is suited to this structure
because it determines the control input by solving a constrained finite-horizon \ac{ocp}.
At each control step, the OCP predicts the response to a sequence of candidate inputs and
minimises a cost over that prediction. Only the first input is applied before the problem is
solved again using the latest measurements~\cite{Rawlings2017,Mayne2000}.

\subsection{Receding Horizon Control}
\label{sec:rhc}

Consider the discrete-time prediction model
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{u}_k),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{f}: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \times \mathbb{R}^{n_u} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \,,
\end{equation*}
%
where $\mathbf{x}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ is the model state and
$\mathbf{u}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_u}$ is the input at step $k$. The function $\mathbf{f}$ is
the one-step state transition. Note that the model state does not have to coincide with the physical state of
the controlled system.

We denote the predicted state and input sequences by
%
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf{x}_{0:H|k} &:= (\mathbf{x}_{0|k}, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{H|k}), \\
    \mathbf{u}_{0:H-1|k} &:= (\mathbf{u}_{0|k}, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{H-1|k}) \,.
\end{align*}
%
Their finite-horizon cost is
%
\begin{equation*}
    J_H\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{0:H|k}, \mathbf{u}_{0:H-1|k}\right)
    = \sum_{j=0}^{H-1} \ell\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{j|k}, \mathbf{u}_{j|k}\right)
    + V_\mathrm{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{H|k}\right) \,,
\end{equation*}
%
where $\ell: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \times \mathbb{R}^{n_u} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the stage cost
and $V_\mathrm{f}: \mathbb{R}^{n_x} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the terminal cost. At step $k$,
the controller computes predicted states and inputs by solving
%
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \min_{\mathbf{x}_{0:H|k},\,\mathbf{u}_{0:H-1|k}} \quad
        & J_H\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{0:H|k}, \mathbf{u}_{0:H-1|k}\right) \\
        \text{s.t.} \quad
        & \mathbf{x}_{0|k} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k, \\
        & \mathbf{x}_{j+1|k}
          = \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{j|k}, \mathbf{u}_{j|k}\right),
          \qquad j = 0, \dots, H-1, \\
        & \mathbf{x}_{j|k} \in \mathbb{X}, \quad
          \mathbf{u}_{j|k} \in \mathbb{U},
          \qquad j = 0, \dots, H-1, \\
        & \mathbf{x}_{H|k} \in \mathbb{X}_\mathrm{f} \,.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:ocp}
\end{equation}
%
The index $j|k$ denotes a quantity predicted $j$ steps ahead using the information available
at step $k$. The initial condition $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$ is the state estimate available to
the controller, and $H \in \mathbb{N}$ is the prediction horizon. % maybe these two sentences can be combined

The sets $\mathbb{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ and $\mathbb{U} \subseteq
\mathbb{R}^{n_u}$ contain the admissible states and inputs. No particular geometry is
assumed for either set. Componentwise bounds and polytopes are common special cases. The set
$\mathbb{X}_\mathrm{f} \subseteq \mathbb{X}$ is the terminal set. Setting
$\mathbb{X}_\mathrm{f} = \mathbb{X}$ removes any additional terminal restriction.

Let $\mathbf{u}^\star_{0|k}, \dots, \mathbf{u}^\star_{H-1|k}$ denote a solution
of~\eqref{eq:ocp}. The controller applies only its first element,
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{u}^\star_{0|k} \,,
\end{equation*}
%
and updates the state estimate when the next measurement becomes available. It then
solves~\eqref{eq:ocp} again over a shifted prediction horizon.
Figure~\ref{fig:receding_horizon} shows
three such solves.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/03_foundations/receding_horizon}
    \caption{The receding horizon principle over three control steps. At each step, the
    controller predicts a trajectory over a prediction horizon of $H$ steps. It applies the
    first planned input and recomputes the remaining sequence after the next measurement.
    The realised trajectory therefore consists of the first transition from successive
    plans. The nominal predictions differ from the disturbed realised trajectory. The
    scalar example uses zero-order-held inputs with the admissible interval
    $\mathbb{U} = [-u_\mathrm{max}, u_\mathrm{max}]$.}
    \label{fig:receding_horizon}
\end{figure}

The finite-horizon solution depends on the state estimate at the beginning of the control
step. After the first input has been applied, the next measurement updates this estimate
before the controller solves a new OCP. This repeated update gives MPC its feedback
character, but does not account for uncertainty within an individual prediction. % last part doesn't really fit in

% maybe we can motivate this part by mentioning the stochastic nature of the JR dynamics
A certainty-equivalent controller replaces uncertain future dynamics by a nominal
prediction, for example by setting future disturbances to their expected value, and solves
the resulting deterministic OCP. This approximation ignores the distribution of future
states. In particular, the cost of the expected state generally differs from the expected
cost when the dynamics or cost are nonlinear. Stochastic MPC evaluates a probabilistic
formulation, while robust MPC enforces performance or constraints for a prescribed
uncertainty set~\cite{Mesbah2016,Hewing2020}.

Closed-loop stability does not follow from the receding-horizon procedure alone. Establishing
stability is especially difficult for nonlinear dynamics and models affected by uncertainty. % citation?
Standard approaches use terminal costs, terminal constraints, and a local terminal control
law. Results without terminal ingredients require additional controllability and
horizon-length assumptions~\cite{Mayne2000,Gruene2017}. The controllers studied here do not
come with such a certificate, so their closed-loop behaviour is assessed in simulation.

\subsection{Linear and Nonlinear MPC}

The numerical class of~\eqref{eq:ocp} depends on the prediction model, the cost functions, %  very abstract and unclear first sentence
and the constraint sets. Consider the affine prediction model
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}_k + \mathbf{c} \,,
\end{equation*}
%
with state matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_x}$, input matrix
$\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x \times n_u}$, and offset
$\mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_x}$. If $\ell$ and $V_\mathrm{f}$ are convex quadratic
functions and the constraint sets are polyhedral, the OCP is a convex \ac{qp}. A positive % I thought the constrainst needed to be affine?
definite quadratic penalty on the input sequence makes the objective strictly convex, so a
feasible problem has a unique solution~\cite{Rawlings2017}.
% why show only the equation for the linear dynamics but not everything else (cost, constraints, etc)?

The Jansen-Rit dynamics are nonlinear because the stimulation drive enters the sigmoid
of~\eqref{eq:sigmoid}. A learned prediction model can nevertheless be linear in its own state.
Koopman theory describes the nonlinear dynamics through a linear operator acting on
observable functions. This operator is generally infinite-dimensional. Exact
finite-dimensional invariant subspaces that retain the variables needed for control exist
only for restricted systems~\cite{Rowley2009,Brunton2016}. Data-driven linear prediction
models therefore use finite-dimensional approximations whose accuracy over the prediction
horizon must be tested.

A nonlinear prediction model usually turns the dynamic equality constraints into a nonconvex
\ac{nlp}. A solver can then converge to different stationary points from different
initial guesses, and global optimality is generally unavailable within a fixed online
computation time~\cite{Findeisen2002}. Linear and nonlinear prediction models therefore
present a trade-off between predictive accuracy and numerical tractability.

\subsection{Transcription and Numerical Solution}
\label{sec:transcription}

Most numerical optimisation algorithms operate on finite-dimensional programs. For a
discrete-time prediction model, direct transcription produces such a program by either
eliminating the predicted states or including them in the decision vector. These choices
lead to direct single and multiple shooting, respectively.

Direct single shooting optimises only the $H n_u$ input components. Define the rollout maps
$\boldsymbol{\varphi}_j$ recursively by
%
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}_0(\mathbf{x})
    &= \mathbf{x}, \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0)
    &= \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0), \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j+1}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0, \dots, \mathbf{u}_j)
    &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(
       \boldsymbol{\varphi}_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{j-1}),
       \mathbf{u}_j\right),
       \qquad j = 1, \dots, H-1 \,. \label{eq:single_shooting}
\end{align}
%
By~\eqref{eq:single_shooting}, the predicted states satisfy
$\mathbf{x}_{j|k} = \boldsymbol{\varphi}_j(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k,
\mathbf{u}_{0|k}, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{j-1|k})$. Substitution in~\eqref{eq:ocp} removes the
state variables and dynamic equality constraints. Every iterate defines a dynamically
consistent rollout of the prediction model, although it does not necessarily satisfy the remaining
constraints.

Direct multiple shooting also optimises the $(H+1)n_x$ predicted state components. It % optimizes over the ... or direct MS also includes the ... in the optimization variables (smt like that)
imposes the one-step continuity constraints~\cite{Bock1984}
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{0|k} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k, \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_{j+1|k} - \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{j|k}, \mathbf{u}_{j|k}\right)
    = \mathbf{0},
    \quad j = 0, \dots, H-1 \,. \label{eq:multiple_shooting}
\end{equation}
%
At an intermediate iterate, the equalities in~\eqref{eq:multiple_shooting} may not hold.
The iterate then consists of
one-step trajectory pieces with gaps between them, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:shooting}.
State constraints act directly on multiple-shooting variables. Under single shooting, they
act on the rollout maps $\boldsymbol{\varphi}_j$. Input constraints retain their original
form in either transcription.

Single shooting also compounds sensitivities across the prediction horizon. At predicted step
$q$, define the Jacobians
$\mathbf{A}_q = \partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{x}
(\mathbf{x}_{q|k},\mathbf{u}_{q|k})$ and
$\mathbf{B}_q = \partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{u}
(\mathbf{x}_{q|k},\mathbf{u}_{q|k})$. For $r < j$,
%
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{j|k}}{\partial \mathbf{u}_{r|k}}
    = \mathbf{A}_{j-1}\mathbf{A}_{j-2}\cdots\mathbf{A}_{r+1}\mathbf{B}_r \,,
\end{equation*}
%
where the matrix product is the identity when $r=j-1$. The same Jacobian product appears
when an iterated prediction model is trained through a multiple-step rollout. Its % mention vanishing/exploding gradients
conditioning depends on the singular values of the factors, so
the product may amplify or attenuate early-input sensitivities. Multiple shooting shortens
these products at the cost of more variables and equality constraints.

\begin{figure}[tb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/03_foundations/shooting}
    \caption{Direct single and multiple shooting over one prediction horizon. Single shooting
    computes one model rollout from the initial state, with only the inputs as decision
    variables. Multiple shooting also treats the state at each interval boundary as a
    decision variable. The gaps in the illustrated intermediate iterate close when the
    dynamic equality constraints are satisfied.}
    \label{fig:shooting}

For a nonlinear prediction model, either transcription gives a finite-dimensional \ac{nlp}.
\Ac{sqp} methods solve a sequence of local quadratic approximations to the Lagrangian and
constraints. Primal-dual interior-point methods solve barrier subproblems while reducing the
barrier parameter~\cite{Waechter2006,Nocedal2006}.

\subsection{Output Feedback and Input-Output Models}
\label{sec:output_feedback}
% where is the mentioning of the state of the NARX model?

The state-feedback formulation~\eqref{eq:ocp} assumes that the model state
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$ is available at the beginning of each control step. This assumption
fails when the controller only measures an output $\mathbf{y}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_y}$. One option
is to reconstruct the state with an observer. For the delayed whole-brain model, however,
the state would contain the $6N$ neural-mass variables and the history required by the delay
terms. Constructing an observer would also require an accurate state-space model.

An input-output model avoids explicit reconstruction of this physical state. A \ac{narx}
model assumes that the recent input-output history contains the information needed to predict
the next output~\cite{Chen1989}. With output-history length
$d_\mathrm{y} \in \mathbb{N}$ and input-history length
$d_\mathrm{u} \in \mathbb{N}$, it has the form
%
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{k+1}
    = \mathbf{f}_\mathrm{NARX}\!\left(
      \mathbf{y}_k, \dots, \mathbf{y}_{k-d_\mathrm{y}+1},
      \mathbf{u}_k, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{k-d_\mathrm{u}+1}\right) \,,
    \label{eq:narx}
\end{equation}
%
In~\eqref{eq:narx}, $\mathbf{u}_k$ is the candidate input for the next sampling interval and the earlier
inputs have already been applied. The input and output histories act as the state of the
prediction model. During a multi-step prediction, the model appends each predicted output
and candidate input before evaluating the next step. The history must be initialized from
recorded measurements and inputs before the first prediction. This construction dispenses
with a separate state estimator, but its validity depends on whether the chosen history is
sufficient for prediction.

\subsection{Cost Function Design}
\label{sec:cost_design}

The stage cost in~\eqref{eq:ocp} commonly separates a state-dependent term from an
input-dependent term,
%
\begin{equation*}
    \ell(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u})
    = \ell_\mathrm{x}(\mathbf{x}) + \ell_\mathrm{u}(\mathbf{u}) \,,
\end{equation*}
%
where $\ell_\mathrm{x}$ measures control performance and $\ell_\mathrm{u}$ penalises control
effort. Their relative scaling determines the trade-off between performance and control
effort.

A tracking cost penalises the deviation from a specified reference. More generally,
economic MPC permits stage costs that are not positive definite about a reference
point~\cite{Ellis2014}. A set-based cost, for example, can vanish throughout an admissible
region and penalise only violations. Such a cost is useful for seizure suppression because the EEG
need not follow one prescribed waveform. Either waveform samples or spectral quantities can
enter $\ell_\mathrm{x}$.

Two common input penalties are
%
\begin{equation}
    \ell_\mathrm{u}^{(1)}(\mathbf{u})
    = w_1\|\mathbf{u}\|_1,
    \qquad
    \ell_\mathrm{u}^{(2)}(\mathbf{u})
    = w_2\|\mathbf{u}\|_2^2 \,,
    \label{eq:input_penalty}
\end{equation}
%
where $w_1 > 0$ and $w_2 > 0$ are weights. For a fixed sampling interval, the sum of the
$\ell_1$ terms over the prediction horizon is proportional to total absolute charge. The
$\ell_2$ term penalises large current magnitudes more strongly, but it is not an
electrical-energy model unless voltage or impedance also enters the formulation. An
$\ell_1$ penalty is convex and nonsmooth at zero.
It can produce sparse solutions, but sparsity is not guaranteed for every OCP. A smooth
approximation removes the nondifferentiability but also changes the optimum.

\subsection{Mixed-Integer MPC}
\label{sec:mi_mpc}

The continuous penalties in~\eqref{eq:input_penalty} do not impose an on-off stimulation
schedule. A binary variable $\delta_{j|k} \in \{0, 1\}$ can indicate whether a nonzero
input is permitted at predicted step $j$. For symmetric per-electrode limits, this condition
can be imposed by
%
\begin{equation*}
    -\delta_{j|k} \, u_\mathrm{max} \leq u_{p,j|k} \leq \delta_{j|k} \, u_\mathrm{max},
    \qquad p = 1, \dots, P, \quad j = 0, \dots, H-1 \,,
\end{equation*}
%
where $u_{p,j|k}$ is the input applied through electrode $p$ and $u_\mathrm{max}>0$ is its
magnitude limit. Setting $\delta_{j|k}=0$ forces every electrode current to zero, whereas
$\delta_{j|k}=1$ retains the continuous input constraints~\cite{Bemporad1999}. The sum
$\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\delta_{j|k}$ counts enabled sampling intervals. It does not count pulses,
because one pulse may extend over several consecutive intervals. A formulation that limits
the number of pulses must instead count transitions from zero to one and specify pulse
duration and shape.

Adding binary variables to a \ac{qp} gives a \ac{miqp}; adding them to a nonlinear OCP gives
a \ac{minlp}. Branch-and-bound partitions the binary assignments and uses relaxations to
bound the objective on each subtree~\cite{Belotti2013}. A convex relaxation supplies a valid
lower bound for minimisation. A local solution of a nonconvex NLP relaxation does not.
Consequently, global certification is substantially harder for a nonconvex \ac{minlp} than
for a convex \ac{miqp}. Blocking the binary variables over groups of steps reduces the
number of binary decisions, but also restricts the admissible schedules~\cite{Cagienard2007}.

Relaxing $\delta_{j|k} \in \{0, 1\}$ to $\delta_{j|k} \in [0, 1]$ produces a continuous
problem. Its solution is not yet a binary schedule. Rounding can change the predicted
trajectory, violate a pulse budget, or destroy feasibility, so the rounded schedule must be
checked in the original constraints. Approximation bounds exist for specific
mixed-integer optimal-control formulations and rounding schemes~\cite{Sager2012}; they do
not apply automatically to an arbitrary discrete-time prediction model.

Continuous optimisation of pulse onset times is a different formulation. A discrete-time
prediction model driven by one current value per sampling interval cannot distinguish two
waveforms that have the same discrete input representation. Sub-sample pulse timing therefore
requires a prediction model or input parameterisation that represents this timing.

Every formulation above depends on the transition function $\mathbf{f}$. The next section
therefore turns to identifying prediction models from input-output data.
