\chapter{Model Predictive Control Framework}\label{ch:mpc_framework}

The models developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:data_driven_predictor_design} predict the response of measured neural activity to a given stimulation sequence.
To use these models for control, the stimulation sequence must be chosen to reduce seizure-related activity while limiting stimulation effort and satisfying current constraints.
This chapter formulates that choice as a finite-horizon optimal control problem.

The chapter first explains how model predictive control uses this problem in a feedback loop and compares two numerical transcriptions.
It then develops the formulation for seizure suppression using the learned predictor, the suppression objective and the current limits.
The chapter closes by examining how costs and constraints can promote sparse stimulation.

\section{General \ac{mpc} Formulation}\label{sec:mpc_formulation}

At each sampling instant, the controller plans a sequence of future inputs by predicting how the state would respond to each candidate sequence.
For these predictions, we consider the discrete-time model
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_k,\mathbf{u}_k)\,,
    \qquad \mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{u}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}\,,
    \label{eq:mpc_prediction_dynamics}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{x}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}$ is the model state and $\mathbf{u}_k\in\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{u}}$ is the input at time step $k\in\mathbb{N}_0$.
The prediction starts from $\mathbf{x}_{0|k}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$, where $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$ is the state available to the controller.
If not measured directly, its estimate can be updated using the latest measurement and the previously applied input~\cite{Findeisen2003}.

The controller plans over $H\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}$ sampling intervals of duration $\Delta t>0$, giving a prediction horizon of duration $T_{\mathrm{pred}}=H\Delta t$.
Using the notation of Chapter~\ref{ch:data_driven_predictor_design}, $\mathbf{u}_{j|k}$ is the input planned at step $k$ for interval $j=0,\ldots,H-1$ and is held constant over that interval.
Starting from $\mathbf{x}_{0|k}$, successive application of~\eqref{eq:mpc_prediction_dynamics} gives the predicted states $\mathbf{x}_{1|k},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{H|k}$ for that input sequence.

\begin{samepage}
Together, the planned inputs and predicted states let the controller assess both the effort a plan requires and the behavior it produces in the model.
The objective encodes these considerations by assigning a cost to the planned inputs and predicted states.
Any limits on the inputs or predicted states enter as constraints that a candidate sequence must satisfy.
The controller therefore minimizes the objective over admissible input sequences, giving the \ac{ocp}~\cite{Rawlings2017}
%
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \min_{\mathbf{u}_{0|k},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{H-1|k}}
        \quad & \mathcal{J}
        = \sum_{j=0}^{H-1}
          \ell(\mathbf{x}_{j|k},\mathbf{u}_{j|k})
          + V_\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{x}_{H|k}) \\
        \text{s.t.}\quad
        & \mathbf{x}_{0|k}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k, \\
        & \mathbf{x}_{j+1|k}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_{j|k},\mathbf{u}_{j|k}),
          \qquad j=0,\ldots,H-1, \\
        & \mathbf{x}_{j|k}\in\mathcal{X},
          \qquad j=0,\ldots,H, \\
        & \mathbf{u}_{j|k}\in\mathcal{U},
          \qquad j=0,\ldots,H-1, \\
        & \mathbf{x}_{H|k}\in\mathcal{X}_\mathrm{f}\,.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mpc_ocp}
\end{equation}
The objective $\mathcal{J}$ sums the stage cost $\ell:\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{u}}\to\mathbb{R}$ over the state and input pairs and adds a terminal cost $V_\mathrm{f}:\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}\to\mathbb{R}$ on the final predicted state.
The sets $\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}$ and $\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{u}}$ specify the admissible states and inputs, respectively and $\mathcal{X}_\mathrm{f}\subseteq\mathcal{X}$ specifies the admissible final states.
\end{samepage}
%
Solving~\eqref{eq:mpc_ocp} once gives an open-loop input sequence based on the information available at step $k$.
Disturbances and model error can cause the actual trajectory to depart from the prediction.
To account for that departure, the controller applies only the first input of an optimal sequence $\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{0|k},\ldots,\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{H-1|k}$,
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{u}_k=\mathbf{u}^{\star}_{0|k}\,.
\end{equation*}
%
At time step $k+1$, the problem is solved again from the updated initial state with the horizon shifted forward by one step.
This procedure is called receding-horizon control.
Although each optimization computes an open-loop input sequence, replanning from updated measurements introduces feedback~\cite{Findeisen2002}.
Figure~\ref{fig:receding_horizon} shows how the plans change as new measurements become available.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics{figures/05_mpc_framework/receding_horizon}
    \caption{Illustration of receding-horizon control for a scalar system.
    Dashed curves show predicted states and candidate inputs, while solid curves show the realized states and applied inputs.
    State and input values are dimensionless.
    Replanning occurs at every sampling instant, with plans at $k=0$, $3$ and $6$ shown for clarity.
    The realized state departs from the nominal forecasts because of disturbances.
    The dotted horizontal line marks the upper input bound.}\label{fig:receding_horizon}

A task may continue indefinitely, but limiting the prediction horizon gives the solver a finite set of decision variables and constraints at each update.
The terminal cost can approximate the cost beyond the horizon~\cite{Rawlings2017,Mayne2000}.
For feedback at the chosen sampling rate, the controller must also solve each finite-horizon problem within one sampling interval.

Replanning alone does not ensure that a feasible plan exists at every subsequent update or that the closed-loop state converges to the target equilibrium~\cite{Rawlings2017,Mayne2000}.
The next section compares two ways to formulate that problem for a numerical solver.

\section{Numerical Transcription and Solution}\label{sec:mpc_numerical_solution}

Direct multiple shooting and direct single shooting are two common ways to transcribe~\eqref{eq:mpc_ocp} for numerical optimization.
They differ in how they represent the predicted states.

\subsection{Direct Multiple Shooting}

In multiple shooting, the predicted states are decision variables alongside the inputs.
The objective and state constraints in~\eqref{eq:mpc_ocp} use the state decision variables directly.
With one-step shooting intervals, the solver adjusts $(H+1)\ndim{x}$ state components together with $H\ndim{u}$ input components and imposes the initial condition and dynamics through the equality constraints~\cite{Bock1984}
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{x}_{0|k} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k, \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_{j+1|k} - \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{j|k}, \mathbf{u}_{j|k}\right)
    = \mathbf{0},
    \quad j = 0, \dots, H-1 \,.
\end{equation*}
%
At an intermediate iterate, a violated dynamics equality appears as a gap between the one-step prediction and the next state decision variable, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:shooting}.

Each dynamics equality couples only two adjacent states and the input between them.
The resulting constraint Jacobian is sparse, with nonzero blocks formed from local model derivatives and identity matrices~\cite{Bock1984,Rawlings2017}.

\subsection{Direct Single Shooting}

In single shooting, only the $H\ndim{u}$ input components are decision variables.
The predicted states are computed by rolling out the model from the given initial state.
For a state $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}}$ and inputs $\mathbf{u}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{u}}$, $i=0,\ldots,H-1$, define the rollout maps $\boldsymbol{\varphi}_j$ recursively by
%
\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}_0(\mathbf{x})
    &= \mathbf{x}, \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0)
    &= \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0), \nonumber \\
    \boldsymbol{\varphi}_{j+1}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0, \dots, \mathbf{u}_j)
    &= \mathbf{f}\!\left(
       \boldsymbol{\varphi}_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}_0, \dots, \mathbf{u}_{j-1}),
       \mathbf{u}_j\right),
       \qquad j = 1, \dots, H-1 \,. \label{eq:single_shooting}
\end{align}
%
The objective and state constraints in~\eqref{eq:mpc_ocp} use the states obtained by evaluating~\eqref{eq:single_shooting} at $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_k$ and the candidate inputs.
The rollout satisfies the initial condition and dynamics at every iterate, leaving only the state and input constraints to enforce explicitly.

An input can affect every subsequent state, so the derivatives of the objective and state constraints with respect to the inputs must account for propagation through the model.

At each offset $j=0,\ldots,H-1$, let $\mathbf{A}_{j|k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}\times\ndim{x}}$ and $\mathbf{B}_{j|k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ndim{x}\times\ndim{u}}$ denote the state and input Jacobians, respectively.
Both derivatives are evaluated at $(\mathbf{x}_{j|k},\mathbf{u}_{j|k})$.
%
\begin{equation*}
    \mathbf{A}_{j|k} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}_{j|k},\mathbf{u}_{j|k})\,,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{B}_{j|k} = \frac{\partial\mathbf{f}}{\partial\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{x}_{j|k},\mathbf{u}_{j|k})\,.
\end{equation*}
%
For $0\le i<j\le H$, repeated application of the chain rule gives
%
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{j|k}}{\partial \mathbf{u}_{i|k}}
    = \mathbf{A}_{j-1|k}\mathbf{A}_{j-2|k}\cdots\mathbf{A}_{i+1|k}\mathbf{B}_{i|k} \,,
\end{equation*}
%
where the product of the state Jacobians is the identity when $i=j-1$.
The product may amplify or attenuate early-input sensitivities, impairing numerical conditioning~\cite{Rawlings2017}.

    Multiple shooting treats the state at each sampling instant as an optimization variable; gaps between the one-step predictions and the next state variables indicate violated dynamics constraints at an intermediate iterate.

\subsection{Computational Tradeoffs}

Both transcriptions use the same input constraints.
Single shooting gives a smaller optimization problem, but its derivatives can involve products across the prediction horizon.
Multiple shooting introduces additional variables and equality constraints with a sparse Jacobian.
The computational tradeoff depends on the dynamics, horizon length and numerical solver.

When the objective or constraints are nonlinear, either transcription gives a constrained \ac{nlp}.
Methods for solving the resulting \ac{nlp} include \ac{sqp}~\cite{Nocedal2006} and interior-point methods, as implemented in IPOPT~\cite{Waechter2006}.
The next section develops the \ac{mpc} formulation for seizure suppression and shows how the learned predictor's history structure reduces the number of decision variables relative to full-state multiple shooting.

\section{\ac{mpc} for Seizure Suppression}\label{sec:mpc_input_output_formulation}

To use the learned input-output predictor for seizure suppression, we first express its history update as a state transition.
The learned map $\mathbf f_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ in~\eqref{eq:recursive_predictor} predicts the next observable, whereas the general \ac{mpc} formulation requires a transition of the complete model state.
For the history state $\mathbf h$ defined in~\eqref{eq:predictor_history}, this transition consists of shifting the stored entries and appending the predicted observable and candidate current, giving
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf h_{j+1|k}
    =\mathbf F_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf h_{j|k},\mathbf u_{j|k})
    =\begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf o_{j-\nhist{o}+2|k}\\
        \vdots\\
        \mathbf o_{j|k}\\
        \mathbf f_{\boldsymbol\theta}(\mathbf h_{j|k},\mathbf u_{j|k})\\[2pt]
        \mathbf u_{j-\nhist{u}+1|k}\\
        \vdots\\
        \mathbf u_{j-1|k}\\
        \mathbf u_{j|k}
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad j=0,\ldots,H-1\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_history_transition}
\end{equation}
%
Identifying $\mathbf x\equiv\mathbf h$, $\mathbf f\equiv\mathbf F_{\boldsymbol\theta}$ and $\hat{\mathbf x}_k\equiv\mathbf h_k$ gives the state-space form in~\eqref{eq:mpc_prediction_dynamics}.
This state-space form permits multiple shooting, but the structure of the history update allows a smaller transcription.

\subsection{\acs{narx} Transcription}

Applying multiple shooting to~\eqref{eq:mpc_history_transition} introduces a history variable at each step and equalities that copy entries between consecutive histories.
Each history can instead be assembled directly from fixed data and the corresponding observable and current decision variables.
Substituting these entries for the history variable eliminates the copying equalities and leaves only the future observables $\mathbf o_{1|k},\ldots,\mathbf o_{H|k}$ and currents $\mathbf u_{0|k},\ldots,\mathbf u_{H-1|k}$ as decision variables.
The dynamics constraint links these variables through the learned predictor,
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf o_{j+1|k}
    =\mathbf f_{\boldsymbol\theta}\!\left(
      \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathbf o_{j-\nhist{o}+1|k}^{\mathsf T} & \cdots & \mathbf o_{j|k}^{\mathsf T} &
        \mathbf u_{j-\nhist{u}|k}^{\mathsf T} & \cdots & \mathbf u_{j-1|k}^{\mathsf T}
      \end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T},\,
      \mathbf u_{j|k}\right),
    \qquad j=0,\ldots,H-1\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_narx_defects}
\end{equation}
%
For integer offsets $s\leq0$, the observable entries are fixed measurements, $\mathbf o_{s|k}=\mathbf o_{k+s}$.
Similarly, entries $\mathbf u_{s|k}=\mathbf u_{k+s}$ with $s<0$ are previously applied currents.
They constitue fixed parameters which supply the initial history for the dynamics constraint in~\eqref{eq:mpc_narx_defects}.
Table~\ref{tab:mpc_transcription_counts} compares the scalar decision-variable and dynamics-equality counts for the three transcriptions.

The transcription specifies how predictions enter the optimization.

\subsection{Control Costs and Input Constraints}\label{sec:mpc_cost_functions}

Regional recruitment cannot be assessed directly from the scalp \ac{eeg} observations available to the controller.
So instead we extract features of predicted \ac{eeg} to serve as proxies for seizure activity and compare that to features extracted from \ac{eeg} signals of healthy simulated brains.
To characterize healthy activity, we use unstimulated simulations in which all regions have the healthy background excitability described in Section~\ref{sec:parameter_selection}.

Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_suppression_references}(a) shows that healthy fluctuations observed through an electrode are small on the scale of seizure oscillations. % chktex 36 -- The panel letter follows the figure number without a space.
This difference motivates approximating the healthy waveform by a constant and penalizing large departures from it.
For each channel, the reference is the mean of the waveform samples pooled across time and across the unstimulated healthy simulations.
The vector of healthy channel means $\bar{\mathbf y}_{\mathrm{healthy}}\in\mathbb R^{\nchan}$ serves as the reference for a standard quadratic tracking cost~\cite{Rawlings2017},
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J_{\mathrm{wave}}
    =\frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=1}^{H}
      \left\|\mathbf y_{j|r}-\bar{\mathbf y}_{\mathrm{healthy}}\right\|_2^2\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_waveform_cost}
\end{equation}
%
Following the recursive prediction notation of Chapter~\ref{ch:data_driven_predictor_design}, $\mathbf y_{j|r}$ denotes the waveform observable $\mathbf o_{j|k}$ with the prediction boundary at waveform sample $k=r$.
The waveform cost in~\eqref{eq:mpc_waveform_cost} penalizes sustained offsets and large oscillations around the healthy mean.
Healthy fluctuations may also contribute to it, so reducing the tracking cost need not selectively attenuate seizure-related activity.

A frequency-specific reference allows the penalty to focus on power that exceeds the range usually observed in healthy recordings.
The reference recordings are transformed into log-power features as described in Section~\ref{sec:spectral_representation}.
For each channel and frequency bin, the empirical 90th percentile across healthy reference frames sets the corresponding threshold in the healthy spectral envelope $\spectral_{\mathrm{healthy}}\in\mathbb R^{\nchan\nbin}$.
A higher percentile leaves more healthy frames unpenalized and also tolerates larger increases in power.

The spectral cost averages the squared excess log power over $\nscore{spec}\in\mathbb N_{>0}$ future frames, all channels and all frequency bins,
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J_{\mathrm{spec}}
    =\frac{1}{\nscore{spec}\nchan\nbin}
      \sum_{j=1}^{\nscore{spec}}\sum_{c=1}^{\nchan}
      \sum_{m=0}^{\nbin-1}
      {\left[\mathcal Y_{j|q,c,m}
      -\mathcal Y_{\mathrm{healthy},c,m}\right]}_+^2\,,
    \label{eq:mpc_spectral_cost}
\end{equation}
%
where ${[a]}_+=\max\{a,0\}$ for $a\in\mathbb R$.
The prediction boundary is spectral frame $q$.
The penalty vanishes at or below the healthy envelope.
Figure~\ref{fig:mpc_suppression_references}(b) illustrates where seizure log power exceeds the envelope at the electrode CP5. % chktex 36 -- The panel letter follows the figure number without a space.

A spectral predictor produces $\spectral_{j|q}$ directly, so~\eqref{eq:mpc_spectral_cost} can be applied to its predicted observables with $\nscore{spec}=H$.
For a waveform predictor, the same spectral transformation used for the reference is applied to the measured waveform history joined to the predicted continuation.
As in the spectral training loss, the measured history supplies the preceding samples required by overlapping segments and temporal smoothing, with $\nscore{spec}\nhop\leq H$.
Each spectral penalty depends on several predicted waveform samples.
Its derivatives therefore propagate through the windowed spectral transformation.

The terminal cost $V_{\mathrm f}$ can use the waveform or spectral penalty at the horizon endpoint, with a weight independent of the corresponding horizon penalty.

To balance predicted seizure suppression against electrode current, the objective includes a squared $L_2$ current cost,
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J_{L_2}
    =\frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}
      \|\mathbf u_{j|k}\|_2^2\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_current_effort}
\end{equation}
%
This cost is proportional to the time integral of squared current over the horizon and serves as a proxy for the supplied electrical energy.
The objective combines the applicable suppression penalties and the $L_2$ current cost with nonnegative weights.

The input constraints follow from the stimulation requirements in Section~\ref{sec:stimulation_interface}.
The current bounds apply to each electrode and Kirchhoff's current law requires the injected and return currents to balance at every interval.
With one control coordinate per physical electrode, the input set is
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal U
    =\left\{\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^{\ndim{u}}:\,
        |u_p|\leq u_\mathrm{max},\ p=1,\ldots,\ndim{u},\quad % chktex 35 -- max names the current bound.
        \sum_{p=1}^{\ndim{u}}u_p=0
      \right\}\,.    \label{eq:mpc_current_constraints}
\end{equation}
%
Predicted activity is penalized through the suppression costs without additional state or terminal-set restrictions, giving $\mathcal X_{\mathrm f}=\mathcal X=\mathbb R^{\ndim{h}}$.
The bounds in~\eqref{eq:mpc_current_constraints} limit instantaneous currents, while the $L_2$ current cost penalizes squared current across the horizon.
The next section introduces penalties and constraints that favor intervals without stimulation.

\section{Sparsity-Promoting Control Formulations}\label{sec:mpc_sparse_formulations}

The squared-current cost penalizes current magnitude.
To also limit how often current is applied, we seek stimulation schedules that suppress predicted pathological activity while leaving some intervals unstimulated.
A schedule is more temporally sparse when it applies current in fewer intervals of the prediction horizon.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$L_1$}{L1} Current Penalty}

A direct way to encourage zero currents is to charge a fixed cost for every nonzero current component.
This count is often called the $L_0$ penalty.
Each current contributes the same amount regardless of its magnitude, so the penalty jumps when a current departs from zero.
The discontinuity makes the count unsuitable for direct use with a solver that requires objective gradients.
A continuous surrogate is the $L_1$ penalty, which sums the absolute current magnitudes.
For the discrete-time maximum hands-off problem, the $L_0$ and $L_1$ formulations have the same minimizers if the $L_1$ problem is normal and has a solution~\cite{Nagahara2016}.
In that setting, normality excludes switching-function values at which the minimizing input is ambiguous.
Here the $L_1$ cost is used to promote sparse currents in an objective that also includes a suppression cost.

For the physical electrode currents, the horizon-averaged $L_1$ cost is
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J_{L_1}
    =\frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\|\mathbf u_{j|k}\|_1
    =\frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\sum_{p=1}^{\ndim{u}}|u_{j|k,p}|\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_l1_cost}
\end{equation}
%
Let $\mathcal J_{\mathrm{supp}}$ denote the selected suppression cost, including its weight.
The continuous-current objective can then be written as
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J_{\mathrm{cont}}
    =\mathcal J_{\mathrm{supp}}
     +w_{L_2}\mathcal J_{L_2}
     +w_1\mathcal J_{L_1}\,,
    \qquad w_{L_2},w_1\geq0\,,
    \label{eq:mpc_sparse_continuous_cost}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal J_{L_2}$ is the $L_2$ current cost from~\eqref{eq:mpc_current_effort}.
Setting either current weight to zero removes its term, so the absolute-current penalty can replace or supplement the $L_2$ current cost.
Near zero, a small feasible increase in current can improve predicted suppression to first order while increasing the $L_2$ current cost only to second order.
The absolute-current cost in~\eqref{eq:mpc_l1_cost} also increases to first order.
If the first-order decrease in weighted suppression cost is smaller than the first-order increase in weighted $L_1$ cost, a sufficiently small change in that direction increases the objective.
The $L_1$ penalty therefore favors keeping a current at zero when the predicted benefit of turning it on is too small to offset the added cost.

The kink that promotes zero currents also makes the $L_1$ term nondifferentiable at those currents.
The gradient-based solvers discussed above use objective derivatives to determine their search directions.
However, the $L_1$ cost can be represented exactly using auxiliary variables and linear inequalities.
For $w_1>0$, introduce real auxiliary variables $z_{j|k,p}$ and replace $\mathcal J_{L_1}$ in~\eqref{eq:mpc_sparse_continuous_cost} with the auxiliary cost $\widetilde{\mathcal J}_{L_1}$, defined by
%
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\mathcal J}_{L_1}
    = \frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\sum_{p=1}^{\ndim{u}}z_{j|k,p}\,,
    \qquad
    -z_{j|k,p}\leq u_{j|k,p}\leq z_{j|k,p}\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_l1_epigraph}
\end{equation}
%
The inequalities in~\eqref{eq:mpc_l1_epigraph} imply $z_{j|k,p}\geq|u_{j|k,p}|$.
Since reducing any slack lowers the objective without changing the currents or predictions, minimization gives $z_{j|k,p}=|u_{j|k,p}|$.
At these values, $\widetilde{\mathcal J}_{L_1}=\mathcal J_{L_1}$.

\subsection{Interval Activation Formulation}

Binary activation variables provide an alternative to the $L_1$ surrogate by counting stimulated intervals directly.
To formulate this interval-level $L_0$ count, we introduce one binary activation variable $\delta_{j|k}\in\{0, 1\}$ shared by all electrodes in each interval.
The current constraints become
%
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        -u_\mathrm{max}\delta_{j|k} % chktex 35 -- max names the current bound.
        &\leq u_{j|k,p}\leq u_\mathrm{max}\delta_{j|k}, % chktex 35 -- max names the current bound.
        \qquad p=1,\ldots,\ndim{u},\\
        \sum_{p=1}^{\ndim{u}}u_{j|k,p}&=0,
        \qquad j=0,\ldots,H-1\,.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mpc_activation_gates}
\end{equation}
%
When $\delta_{j|k}=0$, the bounds force all electrode currents to zero.
When $\delta_{j|k}=1$, the interval is enabled and the original admissible current set applies, including the zero vector.

To favor sparse stimulation, we penalize the fraction of intervals in which stimulation is enabled,
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal J_{\delta}
    =\frac{1}{H}\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\delta_{j|k}\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_activation_cost}
\end{equation}
%
Adding the activation cost~\eqref{eq:mpc_activation_cost} to the continuous-current objective gives the following problem
%
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\mathbf u_{0|k},\ldots,\mathbf u_{H-1|k},\,
          \delta_{0|k},\ldots,\delta_{H-1|k}}
    \quad \mathcal J_{\mathrm{cont}}+w_{\delta}\mathcal J_{\delta},
    \qquad w_{\delta}>0\,,
    \label{eq:mpc_activation_penalty_problem}
\end{equation}
%
subject to~\eqref{eq:mpc_activation_gates}, the dynamics and the initial condition.
For a fixed current sequence, any interval with nonzero current must be enabled.
If activation variables are independent, the activation penalty makes it optimal to disable every interval with zero current.
Thus, minimizing over the activation variables makes $\mathcal J_{\delta}$ equal to the fraction of intervals with nonzero current, a normalized group $L_0$ count.

As an alternative to the penalized problem~\eqref{eq:mpc_activation_penalty_problem}, we can minimize $\mathcal J_{\mathrm{cont}}$ subject to an activation cap,
%
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\delta_{j|k}\leq K\,,
    \label{eq:mpc_activation_cap}
\end{equation}
%
where $K\in\{0,\ldots,H\}$ is the maximum number of enabled intervals.
The cap~\eqref{eq:mpc_activation_cap} and the activation penalty both act on the open-loop plan.
Since only the first current vector of each plan is applied before replanning, neither formulation guarantees a prescribed fraction of unstimulated intervals in the closed-loop trajectory.

\subsection{Solution Approaches for Activation Decisions}

The combination of binary activation decisions and nonlinear predictor dynamics yields a \ac{minlp}.
A standard continuous \ac{nlp} solver cannot enforce the binary restrictions directly.
Solving the \ac{minlp} is generally more computationally expensive than solving the corresponding continuous relaxation because it requires searches over activation schedules.
We therefore discuss three different approaches to handling the binary decisions.

\subsubsection*{Branch and Bound}

Branch and bound is a standard approach to solving a \ac{minlp}~\cite{Bonami2008}.
Its role here can be understood by considering the cost of solving a separate continuous \ac{nlp} for each fixed activation schedule.
Since each of the $H$ intervals has two possible activation values, exhaustive enumeration could require up to $2^H$ such solves at every control update.
The branch and bound algorithm can reduce this count by discarding entire branches of schedules.

The basic branching mechanism starts from a continuous relaxation in which each binary restriction is replaced by $0\leq\delta_{j|k}\leq1$.
If an activation is fractional, the solver splits the schedules into two subsets by fixing that variable to zero in one subproblem and to one in the other.
Repeating these splits produces a search tree whose nodes represent progressively more specified schedules.
The best feasible integer solution found so far is called the incumbent.

The minimum of a relaxed subproblem bounds from below the cost of every binary schedule it represents.
If that bound reaches or exceeds the incumbent cost, the solver can discard the entire subset.
This mechanism can save many \ac{nlp} solves, although each visited node still requires a relaxation solve and the tree may have up to $2^H$ leaves.
Bonmin is the solver used here and its default method follows this approach~\cite{BonminManual}.
With nonconvex learned dynamics, local relaxation minima may give unreliable bounds, so a global certificate would require valid bounds for those nonconvex subproblems.

\subsubsection*{Candidate \ac{mpc} with Grouped Activation Decisions}

Branch and bound seeks to reduce the work of searching the full set of binary schedules.
An alternative is to restrict that set by grouping the horizon intervals into blocks and solving a continuous \ac{nlp} for each block schedule.
The partition is fixed in advance and consists of $B$ contiguous blocks $\mathcal B_1,\ldots,\mathcal B_B$, each containing $H/B$ intervals.
All intervals in a block share one binary decision $d_b$, giving
%
\begin{equation}
    \delta_{j|k}=d_b
    \quad\text{for }j\in\mathcal B_b,
    \qquad d_b\in\{0, 1\},
    \qquad b=1,\ldots,B\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_grouped_activation}
\end{equation}
%
For each of the $2^B$ block schedules defined by~\eqref{eq:mpc_grouped_activation}, fix the activation values and optimize the continuous currents under the predictor dynamics and current constraints.
The activation penalty is constant during each \ac{nlp} solve and enters only when comparing schedules.
Disabled blocks have zero current throughout.
Within an enabled block, each interval's current vector remains independently optimized, so grouping activation decisions does not require holding the current constant over a block.

Let $\mathbf d=(d_1,\ldots,d_B)$ and let $\mathbf u^{(\mathbf d)}$ denote the current sequence returned by a successful candidate solve.
The score combines the continuous objective in~\eqref{eq:mpc_sparse_continuous_cost}, evaluated on the rollout for that candidate, with the activation penalty
%
\begin{equation}
    C(\mathbf d)
    =\mathcal J_{\mathrm{cont}}\!\left(\mathbf u^{(\mathbf d)}\right)
     +\frac{w_{\delta}}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}d_b\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_candidate_score}
\end{equation}
%
With equal block lengths, the fraction of enabled blocks equals the fraction of enabled intervals.
The controller selects the successful candidate with the lowest score in~\eqref{eq:mpc_candidate_score} and applies its first current vector.
The all-disabled schedule fixes the entire current sequence and can be evaluated by a rollout alone.

Grouping into $B<H$ blocks limits the search to at most $2^B$ continuous solves per update, while a finer partition permits more activation timings at the cost of more candidate solves.

\subsubsection*{Continuous Relaxation and Homotopy}

Relaxing the binary decisions to the interval $[0, 1]$ allows a continuous \ac{nlp} solver to optimize the currents and activations jointly.
To recover a binary schedule from this relaxation, we penalize fractional activations, which would otherwise impose intermediate current bounds.
In optimal control, this approach has been applied by solving a sequence of relaxed problems with increasing penalty weights~\cite{Sager2006}.
For the activation schedule $\boldsymbol\delta=(\delta_{0|k},\ldots,\delta_{H-1|k})$, the integrality penalty is
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal P_\beta(\boldsymbol\delta)
    =\beta\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\delta_{j|k}(1-\delta_{j|k}),
    \qquad\beta\geq0\,.
    \label{eq:mpc_integrality_penalty}
\end{equation}
%
For $\beta>0$, each summand in~\eqref{eq:mpc_integrality_penalty} vanishes at both binary values and is positive between them.
The homotopy varies the integrality penalty gradually across a sequence of relaxed problems.
The first solve uses $\beta=0$, so every relaxed schedule has zero integrality penalty.
At each subsequent step, a larger $\beta$ raises the cost of fractional activations and the solver starts from the previous solution to reoptimize the currents and activations.
The sequence ends when a solve satisfies the binary restriction within the solver tolerance or when the continuation limit is reached.
The computational cost depends on the number of continuation solves instead of the size of a search tree.
Small increases in $\beta$ can require more solves, while large increases can make it harder for the solver to follow the solution branch traced by the preceding solves~\cite{Sager2006}.

\section{Chapter Summary}

At each update, the controller uses a learned predictor to choose a stimulation sequence by weighing predicted seizure activity against the current required to suppress it.
The waveform cost measures that activity through squared deviations from each \ac{eeg} channel's healthy mean, so sustained offsets and large oscillations both contribute.
The spectral cost targets elevated power by penalizing squared log-power excess above a healthy threshold for each channel and frequency bin.

The stimulation costs distinguish between the magnitude of the current and how often it is applied.
The quadratic current cost discourages large amplitudes.
To promote sparse stimulation, the controller can use an $L_1$ penalty that favors zero currents or use binary activation to penalize or cap the number of enabled intervals directly in each plan.
The resulting activation decisions can be handled by branch and bound, by enumerating schedules over groups of intervals or by progressively penalizing fractional activations in a continuous relaxation.

The predictor dynamics can enter the optimization through single shooting, full-state multiple shooting or the \acs{narx} transcription.
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments_discussion} compares these design choices in simulation.
